## A note before the first page

A mosaic is made of many pieces. That is a literal statement about material: small pieces of stone, ceramic, or glass are placed so that a larger surface becomes legible. A reader can study the whole composition and still return to an individual tessera. Neither perspective has to destroy the other.

This book begins with Nathan Tabor's **Taborite Model**, a way to organize the experience of reading art by **where it stands in an architectural setting**. The model is deliberately named; it is not presented as a demonstrated universal law used by every ancient artist. It is a proposed interpretive method. A Roman floor mosaic, a Byzantine wall panel, and a dome image may each invite different physical movements of the viewer. The reader must first observe the location, then establish the context, then offer any symbolic comparison.

This book is the first of three short, self-contained volumes accompanying **The Mosaic Reader** research application and **Cipher Quest** learning game.

## Chapter 1 — The first rule: locate the work

Stand before an image and ask a question most visitors forget: **Where was this image meant to be seen?**

Not, initially, what does it mean? Not who hid the code? Not what secret does it reveal? Its placement comes first. A photographed mosaic may have been taken out of a floor and hung on a museum wall. If a reader uses the museum wall to interpret its ancient location, a false coordinate enters the record before the first word is read.

The Mosaic Reader begins by separating **original installation** from **present display**. It asks for the building, archaeological context, site, date or dating range, material, provenance, and an identifiable source. A visitor's photograph matters. So does the publication or catalog that identifies the object.

Now the physical coordinates can be assigned:

- **BELOW:** the floor and what is below the eye line of the original viewer.
- **MIDDLE:** walls, columns, door frames, and eye-level placements.
- **ABOVE:** the vault, ceiling, dome, and whatever must be looked up toward.

These are not synonyms for good and evil. BELOW is not automatically wicked; ABOVE is not automatically innocent. They are interpretive locations anchored to the observable surface.

A floor can preserve the memory of a household; a wall can establish a public story; a ceiling can create an experience of height. The eye and neck participate in that encounter, and the building controls the path through which an image enters attention.

**Field exercise:** Find an image of a mosaic. Write a two-sentence description without proposing meaning. Next record its original surface and the source that establishes it. If original placement is unknown, label it **UNPLACED**. Do not guess.

## Chapter 2 — BELOW: the floor and the foundation

A floor can be crossed, stood upon, and inspected at the viewer's feet. Ancient pavements often depict geometric designs, figures, animals, daily activity, sea life, or mythological events. Their subjects differ widely; their physical position offers a shared coordinate.

In the Taborite Model, BELOW can be read as **earth, foundation, underworld, subconscious**. This is an authorial reading layered onto architecture. The word *subconscious* comes from modern psychological vocabulary; an ancient mosaic maker did not necessarily intend it.

The useful question is what the floor **does**. It supports movement, directs travel, marks thresholds, and sometimes forces a visitor to observe an image while approaching or crossing it. A pattern near a doorway may have a different practical function from a central emblem in a reception room. One material surface may contain several messages.

The reader records motif and observation separately. “A border contains eight repeated forms” is an observation; “the border is a psychological fence” is an interpretation. Both can be kept, but they belong in different fields.

**Reader's record:**

1. **Source:** Describe the photograph or catalog.
2. **Position:** BELOW if originally underfoot.
3. **Visible:** Count the forms and copy any original letters exactly.
4. **Documented:** Record what inscriptions or scholarship actually establish.
5. **Proposed:** Add the Taborite meaning, naming it as a proposed interpretation.

**Field exercise:** Record a floor border with a count you can defend from the image. Now deliberately write two readings: one structural (“a repeated border divides the design”) and one symbolic. Observe the difference between what your eye can verify and what your mind offers.

## Chapter 3 — MIDDLE: the level of encounter

The human eye meets a wall differently from a floor. A wall faces the viewer. A column may guide movement or frame a scene. Text inscribed in the middle register asks to be read from a particular direction. Narrative sequences may travel across walls as the visitor walks.

MIDDLE, in Nathan Tabor's model, is the **conscious present**, a plane of lived experience, choices, public claims, and immediate action. A wall image might represent a ruler, a saint, a political event, or a geometric form. Its position by itself does not verify a single semantic interpretation. Yet the viewer's orientation remains meaningful.

The decoder therefore records three kinds of direction: **the direction of a written text**, **the direction of a depicted narrative**, and **the physical direction from which the viewer approaches**. They may not coincide.

Consider a repeated sign on a column. The sign may be a maker's mark, a letter, a decorative motif, or a later graffito. If the first observation says simply “this is a royal sigil,” the research has already skipped the evidence stage.

The reader's strongest act is to **preserve options rather than erase inconvenient detail**. If three authorities disagree, cite the disagreement. If no one knows, write unknown. The absence of evidence is not evidence that an extraordinary hidden translation has been established.

**Field exercise:** Describe one wall motif in terms of line, curve, angle, count, and location. Then describe it as a possible symbol. Put an explicit label on the boundary between these two accounts.

## Chapter 4 — ABOVE: the viewer tilts upward

A ceiling image changes posture. It can sit beyond ordinary arm's reach. The dome, apse vault, and ceiling are surfaces that may direct the eye toward an architectural center. Gold-ground mosaics can interact with light; glass tesserae may flash as the observer changes position.

The Taborite Model places these visual events in **ABOVE**: sky, heavens, cosmos, universe, supra-consciousness.

Again, the physical situation and the conceptual reading should remain distinct. A dome may represent heaven within a particular religious program; that case requires studying the monument's history and iconography. Another high ceiling might depict a secular emblem. Nothing in height alone licenses a universal translation.

Above is therefore a **questioning coordinate**. What scene is at the highest point? What images surround it? Are there concentric circles, radial divisions, rows, or inscriptions? What is the light source? Could restoration have changed the colors? Was the viewer intended to stand beneath the center or travel through a procession?

**Field exercise:** Sketch a dome as circles and bands. Do not draw faces or invent human figures. Mark each observable line and count each repeated form. Then place the physical findings in one column and the Taborite reading in another.

## Chapter 5 — The five-stage reader

The physical placement system gains practical strength when joined to a five-stage evidence protocol inspired by Ogham's visible geometry.

**Stage 1 — SPINE.** Identify the axis: stone edge, ruled line, register, column, tile grid, or another observed carrier. When none is visible, write “not established.”

**Stage 2 — ARMS.** Record the direction or gesture of the marks. Do strokes reach left or right, cross a boundary, appear as centered notches, or form shapes unlike Ogham? This is an analogy for non-Ogham materials, not proof they share an ancient script.

**Stage 3 — COUNT.** Count what is actually observable. Five repeated grooves is five grooves; it is not automatically five syllables or five invisible impulses.

**Stage 4 — ROOT.** Determine the actual letter value, word, language, source pronunciation, or documented association when available. An environmental anchor like oak, birch, or river may be a useful interpretive layer, but should not be substituted for a linguistic etymology without evidence.

**Stage 5 — CONTEXT.** Add source, dating, object history, and any interpretive claim under its own label.

The stages are repeatable. A second reader can return to the same object, inspect the same original image, and compare the records without inheriting a hidden assumption.

## Chapter 6 — One tessera, two ledgers

Imagine a museum floor panel containing a central circle, four smaller corner circles, and a surrounding border. A reader may begin with a bold theory: “The four corners symbolize four realms surrounding a central world.” That could be useful as a proposal. But first comes the measurement: five circles, their relative sizes, the material, the placement, the object catalog, and the fact that none of the circles is writing.

The Mosaic Reader uses **two linked ledgers**:

| Evidence ledger | Interpretation ledger |
|---|---|
| Original sign or visible feature | Proposed Taborite reading |
| Measurable line, count, position | Symbolic association |
| Original inscription copied unchanged | Modern paraphrase or analogy |
| Source, date, uncertainty | Author and rationale of interpretation |
| Alternative specialist readings | Comparative patterns and open questions |

These do not compete. The first protects the artifact. The second gives the investigator space to think. The boundary is where a reliable universal reader begins.

The system's arithmetic is written here as the author's rule of integrity: **1 + 1 = 2 — BUILD; 1 − 1 = 0 — CLEAR. YES is YES — NO is NO.** The practical software interpretation is equally direct: adding an interpretation adds a record; clearing a proposed interpretation never destroys the original source.

## Chapter 7 — A complete field session

Select a photograph of a mosaic known to have been installed in a floor. Enter the project title, approximate date if supported, current collection, and source. Click a visible detail to place a marker.

In the annotation, choose BELOW. Copy any inscription in the source script, or leave the inscription field blank if there is none. Enter an observation such as “three parallel dark strokes cross a pale border.” Give the three strokes an observed count. Mark the orientation as observed rather than inferred. If you think it resembles Ogham, write **“geometric comparison to Ogham”**; do not silently rename the mosaic marks an ancient Ogham inscription.

Now add one proposed symbolic reading. Enter a confidence or evidence label. Export the JSON project. Open the exported text and confirm that the original and the proposed reading have distinct fields. A second person can now see what you saw and what you concluded.

This is how the app becomes a research notebook rather than an oracle.

## Closing — From stone to the alphabet

The first volume teaches one discipline: **read the place before assigning the message**. A mosaic exists in a building, an excavation, a culture, and a history. Its modern interpretation can be imaginative while its evidence remains intact.

### Glossary

**Tessera:** One small piece in a mosaic.  
**Provenance:** An object's documented history and origin.  
**Register:** A horizontal or otherwise organized band of imagery or text.  
**Spine:** In this research method, the observed axis of the marks.  
**Interpretive plane:** BELOW, MIDDLE, or ABOVE within the Taborite Model.  
**Transcription:** Recording the original sequence in readable signs without translating it.  
**Hypothesis:** An explanation proposed for testing, not a documented fact.
