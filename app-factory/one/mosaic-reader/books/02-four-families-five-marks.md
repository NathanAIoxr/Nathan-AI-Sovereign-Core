## Opening — An alphabet that tells you where to look

An Ogham inscription is not arranged like a printed paragraph in modern English. Its basic written forms rely on strokes in groups positioned relative to a line or stone edge. On many monuments the edge of the stone is the physical axis. A reader who copies isolated signs but ignores the edge may lose essential information.

Ogham is a writing system, not merely an allegory. Scholars identify its primary twenty letters in **four sets of five**, commonly called *aicmí*. The signs differ by orientation and count. Later manuscript traditions supply additional signs known as *forfeda*. The script gives this book a strong structural starting point because its geometry is explicit and teachable.

The Mosaic Reader uses the structure as a **comparison method**, with the historical script and Nathan Tabor's symbolic interpretations stored in different layers.

## Chapter 1 — The spine and the movement of a reader

Start by standing beside an inscribed stone in imagination. An edge rises from the ground. One sign follows another along the edge. Original orientation matters: many monumental Ogham inscriptions are read upward; horizontal manuscript transcriptions ordinarily run left to right. The exact line path, the stone's surviving condition, and possible continuation onto another edge must be established case by case.

The “spine” need not be inked. The physical edge can function as the line that defines the signs. A mark on one side of the edge is not equivalent to one cut crossing it. In a manuscript, a drawn guide line can take over that role.

The first movement in Cipher Quest is therefore not “translate.” It is **find the spine**. The next movement is **count**. Only after those two steps does the letter value become legible.

This is a lesson for every writing tradition. Record the carrier and orientation before proposing a meaning. For the Latin alphabet, an invisible writing line may provide a useful modern analogy; for a Chinese character, an enclosing square may be a useful graphic frame; for an Egyptian inscription, placement and facing direction may matter. Those are different systems, and the analogy does not turn them into Ogham.

## Chapter 2 — The first family: five strokes on one side

The first Ogham group follows this traditional letter order:

| Coordinate | Sign | Approximate conventional Latin value | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1:1 | ᚁ | B | Beith |
| F1:2 | ᚂ | L | Luis |
| F1:3 | ᚃ | F | Fearn |
| F1:4 | ᚄ | S | Saille |
| F1:5 | ᚅ | N | Nion / Nin |

Each family member is a **count within a stroke orientation**. In the app's conceptual language, the first family occupies the “right-side” branch. The original right/left appearance depends on transcription direction and display convention, so the physical artifact remains decisive.

The traditional letter names are not licenses to assign a single unchanging mystical meaning to every inscription. Later learned word lists, tree associations, and modern symbolic traditions overlap but are not identical. The user-supplied interpretive layer connects Beith to birch/renewal, Luis to rowan/protection, Fearn to alder/defense, Saille to willow/flexibility, and Nion to ash/bridging worlds. In the Reader, those belong under **symbolic associations**.

**Quest exercise:** Find ᚄ on the chart. Say its letter value, family, and count: **S, family one, count four**. That answer can be checked without claiming the sign means “willow” in every context.

## Chapter 3 — The second family and the correction that matters

The diagonal/across-stroke family is historically ordered:

| Coordinate | Sign | Conventional value | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| F2:1 | ᚆ | H | Uath |
| F2:2 | ᚇ | D | Dair / Duir |
| F2:3 | ᚈ | T | Tinne |
| F2:4 | ᚉ | C | Coll |
| F2:5 | ᚊ | Q | Ceirt |

This **H–D–T–C–Q** order is the reference for the game. An early source draft placed D first in the group; the original draft is preserved as source evidence, while the executable chart uses the traditional sequence. Both facts can be documented without silently rewriting the original.

Why does one coordinate matter? If D is assigned the wrong count, every result derived from that count becomes unreliable. In a teaching game, players can learn the wrong symbol. In a research notebook, that error may look like a meaningful discovery merely because its formatting is confident.

The model's arithmetic rule is useful here: **1 + 1 = 2 — BUILD. 1 − 1 = 0 — CLEAR.** The app adds a corrected record rather than erasing the source. “Correct” means supported by the referenced signary, not approved merely because it sounds pleasing.

**Quest exercise:** Read ᚇ. The answer is **D, family two, two strokes**. Compare ᚈ: **T, family two, three strokes**.

## Chapter 4 — The third and fourth families

The third family provides another direction of side strokes:

| Coordinate | Sign | Conventional value | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| F3:1 | ᚋ | M | Muin |
| F3:2 | ᚌ | G | Gort |
| F3:3 | ᚍ | NG | Ngeadal |
| F3:4 | ᚎ | ST / Z (varies in transcription) | Straif |
| F3:5 | ᚏ | R | Ruis |

The fourth consists of vowels, shown as notches or marks on the baseline in common transcriptions:

| Coordinate | Sign | Conventional value | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| F4:1 | ᚐ | A | Ailm |
| F4:2 | ᚑ | O | Onn |
| F4:3 | ᚒ | U | Ur |
| F4:4 | ᚓ | E | Eadhadh |
| F4:5 | ᚔ | I | Iodhadh |

Do not assume a modern English spelling is a faithful phonetic transcript of early Irish sounds. Alphabetic equivalence is a convenience; sound systems change across centuries. The value of the chart lies in its named **sign, family, count, and conventional transliteration**.

In Nathan Tabor's interpretive model, the fourth family also offers an analogy for centered resonance in speech. That comparison may help students remember that vowels often allow relatively unimpeded airflow. It is not a law stating that every vowel in every language corresponds to an Ogham notch.

**Quest exercise:** Decode ᚐᚑᚒᚓᚔ into the instructional sequence **A O U E I**. Now read the same five signs in reverse. The second reading is an exercise in sequence manipulation, not evidence of a hidden ancient reversed message.

## Chapter 5 — The later forfeda: do not flatten the variants

Five frequently taught later characters are:

| Sign | Common reading | Common name |
|---|---|---|
| ᚕ | EA | Eabhadh / Koad in some teaching traditions |
| ᚖ | OI | Óir |
| ᚗ | UI | Uilleann |
| ᚘ | IA / IO | Ifín |
| ᚙ | AE | Eamhancholl |

The supplement is not as historically uniform as the foundational twenty. A manuscript, scholarly edition, or Unicode name list may categorize or name an item differently. A sixth additional Unicode sign, **ᚚ**, is Peith.

The app displays later symbols separately from the main four-by-five grid. This is deliberate. A compact “25-letter master chart” is pedagogically useful, but presenting the five extras as if they were formed by an identical fifth family of ordinary Ogham strokes would misrepresent their history.

A well-designed decoder keeps alternate values in a visible ledger. For an inscription containing an ambiguous supplementary mark, the correct action may be to **leave more than one candidate** until context narrows the reading.

## Chapter 6 — TABOR through the modern substitution exercise

Write the modern Roman letters **T A B O R**. Substitute each with the closest one-letter instructional Ogham character:

- **T** → ᚈ → F2:3
- **A** → ᚐ → F4:1
- **B** → ᚁ → F1:1
- **O** → ᚑ → F4:2
- **R** → ᚏ → F3:5

The result is **᚛ᚈᚐᚁᚑᚏ᚜**. The feather-like opener and closer are the Ogham bracket marks. They are part of the teaching display, not proof that the personal or family name was spelled this way in an ancient inscription.

A useful decoder asks three more questions. What historical language are we trying to represent? Does the historical sound inventory map one-to-one to modern English letters? Is there a surviving source that actually writes this name? Here, there is no supplied ancient source. The output is therefore labeled **modern character substitution**, not “the ancient Irish translation of TABOR.”

This exercise is foundational to the game's ethics: an operation can be correct at its own level and still not prove a larger historical conclusion.

## Chapter 7 — Make a game from the grid

A learner can master Ogham in stages.

**Quest One: The Alphabet.** A single sign appears. Select its conventional letter value. The player learns visual recognition and can consult the chart.

**Quest Two: The Stroke Grid.** The player identifies the family and mark count. This is the closest activity to an Ogham coordinate cipher.

**Quest Three: Three Worlds.** A mosaic's original position is described; the player chooses BELOW, MIDDLE, ABOVE, or UNPLACED using the Taborite Model's terminology.

**Quest Four: Evidence Vault.** The player is shown a statement and must distinguish observation, source citation, letter substitution, and symbolic interpretation.

**Quest Five: Master Decoder.** All previous categories mix. Winning requires accuracy and evidence discipline rather than an unsupported claim of universal hidden knowledge.

The app keeps local XP and best scores. A player can export progress as JSON and inspect what was recorded. No leaderboard or account is required. It is a game, but the original subject is treated seriously.

## Chapter 8 — From Ogham to other scripts

An Ogham sign encodes a coordinate within its own system. It does not follow that a Chinese character, a runic inscription, and a Mayan glyph use the same coordinate scheme. To extend the method responsibly, separate **universal questions** from **language-specific answers**.

Universal questions include: Where is the original text? Which direction does it run? What do we see? How many things can be counted? What evidence establishes a phonetic value? Which meanings are proposed rather than documented?

Language-specific answers require knowledge of that writing system and its historical stages. Some signs represent consonants, some syllables, some words, some semantic classes, and some combinations. Not every writing system is alphabetic, and some inscriptions remain undeciphered.

The Mosaic Reader is a **universal comparative notebook**, not a magic key that automatically translates every script. It can accept Unicode from many languages, preserve unsupported signs, and record the necessary observations. The reader's job is still to research.

## Closing — The final exercise

Imagine three inscriptions: one copied accurately, one guessed from its shape, and one accompanied by a dated catalog. Which has the strongest evidence? The answer depends on what claim is being evaluated. A careful transcription establishes visible marks. A catalog establishes attribution or context to the extent the source is trustworthy. A resemblance may generate a hypothesis; it cannot independently authenticate a translation.

The strength of the Mosaic Reader comes from its rule: **preserve the original and label the conclusion**.

### Glossary

**Ogham:** Early medieval writing system found in inscriptions and later learned traditions.  
**Aicme:** A group/family of Ogham letters.  
**Forfeda:** Later or supplemental Ogham characters.  
**Transliteration:** Rendering signs in a different writing system, not necessarily translating their language.  
**Stroke coordinate:** The family and mark count used to describe a primary Ogham sign.  
**Cryptographic analogy:** A teaching model used to compare structures, not historical proof of secret transmission.
