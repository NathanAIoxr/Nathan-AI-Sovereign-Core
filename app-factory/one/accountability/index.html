WHY WERE SO MANY ACCUSED PRIESTS NEVER CRIMINALLY CHARGED?

Chapter 1 — The Question That Was Buried

“Why are pedophile priests not arrested?” The author's challenge is aimed at a system in which a report of child abuse could be treated as a personnel matter rather than an urgent child-safety matter. The core demand is simple: preserve evidence, alert civil authorities, protect children, investigate the allegation and prosecute where the evidence and law support charges.

For decades, abuse survivors described experiences that church authorities did not adequately confront. The Pennsylvania grand jury's 2018 investigation identified widespread abuse and institutional concealment. A transfer could move an accused priest away from one parish without resolving whether children at another parish were safe.

Chapter 2 — The Numbers Behind “Not Arrested”

The John Jay College study of U.S. Catholic clergy allegations from 1950–2002 counted 4,392 priests and deacons with allegations not withdrawn or known to be false. Church-supplied records reported 384 criminally charged and 252 convicted; the study also reported at least 100 who served prison time. Only roughly nine percent of the accused clergy were recorded as criminally charged in that historical dataset.

These figures describe charges, convictions and prison time, not the exact number arrested. A nationwide cumulative arrest count through 2026 has not been established by this source. The absence of a precise arrest total must not be used to minimize the huge gap between the number accused and the number criminally charged.

Chapter 3 — Moving the Danger

When leaders concealed credible warnings or quietly reassigned priests, they could expose new communities to danger. The Pennsylvania grand jury documented concealment and the movement of accused clergy. Some perpetrators abused more than one child; in some cases the institutional response enabled access to additional potential victims.

Moving a particular accused person does not, by itself, prove that person committed a new offense. But transferring someone without adequate reporting, restrictions and protective action can perpetuate risk. The author's question remains: why was the instinct to relocate ever allowed to outrun the responsibility to protect?

Chapter 4 — Five Billion Dollars: Follow the Categories

A 2025 Georgetown University CARA summary of church surveys documented $5,025,346,893 in U.S. clergy-abuse-allegation-related expenditures during 2004–2023. Its breakdown: $3,552,766,588 in settlements to victims; $195,813,340 in other victim payments; $293,270,410 in support for offenders; $875,733,512 in attorneys' fees; and $107,763,043 in other costs.

The total is not a documented budget for moving accused priests. Victim compensation should not be equated with transferring alleged offenders. It is precisely because the categories are so different that the expenditures deserve scrutiny: who was protected, when, how, and at what cost to survivors?

Chapter 5 — What ChatGPT Got Wrong

When the author demanded arrest numbers, ChatGPT repeatedly emphasized that “many” priests had been arrested without establishing a nationwide count. The wording was imprecise and misleading because it obscured how few of the accused in the historical study were criminally charged. The author characterized that failure with the headline “CHATGPT LIED.”

The supported audit finding is that ChatGPT made an inadequately supported claim and framed the response poorly; intentional deception or an active cover-up by ChatGPT has not been established. This is an example of the distinction between a documented error, its impact, and an allegation about intent. The correction should lead with the prosecutorial gap and sources, not retreat into word games.

Chapter 6 — Congress, Religion and Responsibility

The author explicitly calls on Representative Virginia Foxx of North Carolina and Representative Robert Aderholt of Alabama to answer questions about oversight, child-safety enforcement, reporting laws and transparency. Their mention is a request for accountability, not evidence that either lawmaker authorized the church's $5 billion in expenditures or priest transfers. Such claims require separate documentary proof.

“WELCOME TO THE CHRISTIAN NATION OF AMERICA” is the author's indictment of the distance between a society's proclaimed values and the protection actually afforded to children. Its sharper moral accusation is directed at the contradiction between professing care for children and tolerating the concealment of abuse.

Chapter 7 — The Real Assignment

Protect the child first. Do not substitute a reassignment or internal review for lawful reporting. Preserve evidence, take survivors seriously, support independent investigations, and require public institutions to explain the laws and systems that failed. A meaningful conclusion is measured not by a slogan but by safer children, accountable offenders, informed communities and fair legal proceedings.

Accountability questions for public officials

Virginia Foxx (NC) and Robert Aderholt (AL): What protections are in place for mandatory reporting to civil authorities? How can investigations continue when alleged crimes are historical? What oversight and victim-support measures would you support? Which agencies are responsible for follow-through?

No evidence is presented here that either representative directly approved church transfers or controlled the church's reported costs.

How to read the case honestly

DOCUMENTED Transfers and institutional concealment occurred in cases investigated by the Pennsylvania grand jury; historical data show limited criminal charging relative to the accusations.

AN AUTHOR'S CLAIM The transfer pattern represents a betrayal of professed Christian values; ChatGPT's misleading framing is described by the author as a lie.

NOT ESTABLISHED That every accused priest reoffended after a transfer; a precise nationwide count of priest arrests; or that named members of Congress funded or ordered the transfers.
