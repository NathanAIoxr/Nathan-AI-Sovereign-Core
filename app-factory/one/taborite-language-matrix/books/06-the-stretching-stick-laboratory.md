# Volume 6 — The Stretching-Stick Laboratory

**The Taborite Universal Language Matrix — by Nathan Tabor**

*Turning static geometry into a playable visual lesson*

> First-edition short manuscript. Research and interpretation are separated; see the series notes.

## Chapter 1: A Figure Made of Lines

The user's memorable comparison is a car-lot inflatable tube figure: one central body and arms thrown outward. Ogham, seen beside such a figure, offers a simple visual analogy for the direction of strokes. The historical alphabet remains a system of writing, not a literal record of human anatomy.

A game can exploit the visual resemblance without presenting it as scholarship. The player chooses a family and a number from one through five. A line drawing shifts to the correct configuration. Then the corresponding historical glyph appears.

The comparison gives beginners a reason to notice orientation. Once orientation is learned, the player can read a sequence of letters without relying on the analogy.

## Chapter 2: The Challenge Board

Begin at level one with the first family. Let the learner identify the glyph, hear or read the letter label, and inspect the stroke count. At level two, mix the first two families. At level three, include all four. A later challenge introduces the forfeda as an expansion with different rules.

Award points only for a completed answer, not merely for pressing a button. Display the correct response after an error, and allow the next question without punishment. Store personal bests in the local browser rather than transmitting them to an account.

For an accessibility check, ensure every challenge can be answered without color or animation.

## Chapter 3: The Ethics of Play

The object of the game is literacy and curiosity. It should never imply that guessing a poetic meaning is the same as translating an inscription. Questions about known characters should have fixed answers; interpretive questions should be clearly marked as open discussion.

Keep the visuals textual and geometric. No portraits or invented historical people are necessary. The player studies traces of movement while the original writing system remains the authority for the answer.
