# Volume 7 — Ogham, Runes, and Other Roads

**The Taborite Universal Language Matrix — by Nathan Tabor**

*A comparison of similarities that does not invent a common origin*

> First-edition short manuscript. Research and interpretation are separated; see the series notes.

## Chapter 1: The Angular Question

Several ancient scripts use straight or angular strokes. Elder Futhark is a Germanic runic alphabet; Ogham is an early medieval script strongly associated with Irish-language inscriptions. They differ in character sets, reading conventions, geographic histories, and letter forms.

A particular rune may resemble a stick figure with its arms raised. That resemblance is visually real. It does not demonstrate that the rune and a given Ogham character had the same pronunciation, name, or sacred function.

Compare scripts by constructing two tables: one for observable geometry, another for attested linguistic and historical facts. Similar shapes can be recorded while the origin question remains separate.

## Chapter 2: The Cross-Script Classroom

Bring Tifinagh, Old Turkic, and other scripts into the same viewing space. Each retains its own alphabet, script direction, Unicode block when applicable, historical period, and research citations. A geometry classifier can count lines, bends, loops, and intersections across them.

Those geometric counts are descriptive features. A written sign cannot be translated merely because it resembles another sign. Chinese characters, Maya glyphs, and syllabaries encode language in ways that need their own linguistic data.

A universal system has to respect difference. It is universal in the kinds of observations it stores, not because it derives every tradition from one invented key.

## Chapter 3: The Meeting of Traditions

Medieval Irish manuscripts preserve several special Ogham forms and discussions of other writing traditions. Such records are valuable historical evidence for scholarly comparison. They should be read as evidence of contact or learned classification only when the manuscript supports that claim.

The cross-script ledger should link each asserted relationship to a source. It can display confirmed borrowing, possible influence, coincidental geometry, and independent invention as separate statuses.

The reader leaves this volume with a method: search for the connection, cite what survives, and refuse to manufacture the missing bridge.
