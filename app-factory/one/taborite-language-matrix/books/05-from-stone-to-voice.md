# Volume 5 — From Stone to Voice

**The Taborite Universal Language Matrix — by Nathan Tabor**

*Articulation, transcription, and the sound of a written mark*

> First-edition short manuscript. Research and interpretation are separated; see the series notes.

## Chapter 1: A Letter Is Not a Breath

Writing records language through conventional signs. Speech takes place through moving air, vibrating or non-vibrating vocal folds, the tongue, lips, jaw, and other parts of the vocal tract. There is no one-to-one physical law equating an Ogham right-hand stroke with a bilabial sound in every language.

For speech analysis, begin with a recording and a documented transcription. The International Phonetic Alphabet provides symbols for many contrasts in speech: place of articulation, manner, voicing, vowel height and backness, and other features. It is a linguistic notation, not a claim that all human languages use the same inventory.

The Taborite matrix can place those measurements in a coordinate view while keeping the source IPA transcription unchanged.

## Chapter 2: The Ear and the Mouth

Consider English OAK. The spelling contains three Latin letters; a common pronunciation has a vowel followed by a velar stop. Written spelling and spoken articulation are different descriptions of the same word. They should not be collapsed into one record.

For a multilingual project, enter the orthographic form, a language identifier, an IPA transcription where documented, and an audio sample where permission allows. Record dialect and speaker details only when appropriate and consented to. Do not infer an ancient pronunciation from a modern English spelling.

A speaker can then compare the tongue's location with the direction of Ogham strokes as a learning metaphor, without claiming a historical causal relationship.

## Chapter 3: The Voice Laboratory

Build lessons where the player hears a sound, sees an IPA symbol, and then inspects its features. A transcription may be uncertain. Speech-recognition systems may be unavailable, may require network access, and may perform badly on minority or ancient languages.

The universal codec should encode a transcript as text even when no speech model supports the language. That separates the success of preserving text from the difficult scientific task of recognizing speech.

The laboratory's achievement is reproducibility: the same input symbols create the same reversible output. It is not a promise to translate every recorded voice.
