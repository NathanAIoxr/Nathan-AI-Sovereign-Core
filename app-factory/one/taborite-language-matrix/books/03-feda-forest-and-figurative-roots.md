## Chapter 1: Names Are Not Dictionaries

Ogham letter names are known through medieval Irish scholarship and literary tradition. Some have direct botanical associations, such as Beith with birch and Dair with oak. Other names are more difficult: Muin has been connected with the neck, while Gort is associated with a field. Later interpretations often supply a comprehensive tree system.

A tree reading can be a rich poetic experience without being the literal translation of a carved inscription. A sequence of signs spelling a person's name is not thereby a literal list of plants. Letter names and word meanings operate at different levels.

The Taborite reader can preserve this layered richness by recording the traditional name, the scholarly gloss, and the modern symbolic theme in three distinct fields.

## Chapter 2: The Forest of Associations

The birch may become a metaphor for a new beginning, the oak for endurance, and the yew for long memory. These can be useful themes in a poem, novel, or learning game. They do not come automatically from the sound values B, D, and I.

Compare the method with writing an acrostic. The initial letters create one structure, while the complete words create another. A poet may intentionally relate the two. Without evidence of that intention, the relationship remains a reading by the later interpreter.

A well-designed educational app can show several interpretations at once: the letter sound, a historically documented name, a botanical tradition where supported, and a user-authored symbolic reading.

## Chapter 3: The Root Ledger

Create a ledger of each of the twenty characters. Add a primary-language or manuscript citation for every historical claim. Give the strongest evidence a visible status, and keep disputed etymologies open for revision. The ledger must allow more than one hypothesis rather than overwrite the older one.

When developing the Taborite Model, add a fourth column titled Original Interpretation by Nathan Tabor. This column can host the connection to stretching figures, cycles of nature, architectural orientation, and the three planes of experience.

The ledger becomes a bridge between scholarship and creation because it shows precisely where documentary evidence ends and a new composition begins.
