# THE TABOR SECRET SOCIETY
## BOOK I — BEFORE THE FIRST ROAD

Series by Nathan Tabor

Narrator: John Nathan Tabor, a man from Alabama. First-person fictional portrayal; he/him.

**OUR WORD IS OUR BOND**

*Manuscript development draft · Chapters 1–6 · New AI-assisted prose in the requested sweet, sharp Southern register. Based on the saved series bible; this draft does not replace the author's original words. The modern investigation and the ancient narrative it uncovers are fictional.*

## Chapter One — The Family Discount

The man wanted forty thousand dollars for a box of papers and acted offended when I asked whether the box came with a truck.

It was a nice box. Cedar, brass corners, a lid somebody had polished until it reflected the overhead lights. But I had seen nice boxes before. You can put a rotten thing in cedar and improve the smell without improving the contents.

“Mr. Tabor, you're looking at your inheritance.”

“Then why am I buying it?”

He straightened the cuff of his shirt. Vernon Pike was one of those fellows whose clothes appeared to have a separate income. Everything about him fit except the answer.

“Recovery has costs.”

“So does a tow truck. They still tell you where they picked up the car.”

Across the table, Ada lowered her glasses far enough to give me the look. She had agreed to inspect the papers, not referee a wrestling match in the private room of an antique shop.

I smiled at her. She did not return it. Ada could make silence feel like a school principal had pulled up a chair.

Pike opened the box.

Inside lay a packet tied with narrow tape. He reached for it with a theatrical slowness that suggested all human history had been waiting for him to finish the gesture.

“Before the roads,” he said. “Before the names were changed. Before your people were written out.”

There it was. My people. Two words laid across the table like bait.

He knew my name. He knew which part of Alabama I came from. He knew I had been following old records, old spellings, old stones, and the things families carry without knowing who first handed them over.

He knew enough to aim.

Ada held out her hand.

“The ownership records first.”

“You'll understand the importance when you see the manuscript.”

“I'll understand the ownership when I see the ownership records.”

I leaned back. Well, all right. She had brought her own chair to the wrestling match.

Pike produced a folder. Three sales descriptions. Two letters mentioning a private collection. A photograph of the cedar box taken on a different table. None of it reached back farther than a few years.

Ada arranged the pages in date order.

“Who held it before this?”

“The family requested discretion.”

“Which family?”

“That's confidential.”

“Then that's where our record stops.”

He looked at me, inviting me to join him in being disappointed with her.

I have been invited into plenty of things. A man can decline.

“Show her the paper,” I said.

He untied the packet.

The first leaf had no date. No signature. A border of dark marks surrounded the writing, and at the bottom was a seal shaped like a star with thirteen points. Under the seal, a later hand had written five words in English.

**OUR WORD IS OUR BOND.**

I stopped smiling.

Pike saw it. His whole face settled, comfortable as a cat that had heard the pantry latch.

“You recognize it.”

“I recognize the words.”

“And what they mean for your family.”

Ada had not looked up. She was examining the edges without touching the writing.

“Mr. Pike, where is the analysis establishing the date?”

“It's in preparation.”

“The translator's name?”

“The translation came with the collection.”

“Then who identified the script?”

Pike put both hands on the table.

“Some truths aren't available through ordinary channels.”

“Well, sir,” I said, “your invoice sure is.”

Ada closed her notebook.

We weren't buying anything that afternoon.

Pike gave me a copy of the English rendering anyway. On the cover, in handsome type, it said BEFORE THE FIRST ROAD. He had printed my surname beneath the title.

“Read it before you decide.”

I took the copy. I wasn't giving him forty thousand dollars. I also wasn't going to pretend those five words had failed to reach me.

That's how a hook works. Knowing it is a hook doesn't mean you didn't feel the tug.

## Chapter Two — Coffee Without a Pedigree

Ada's workroom occupied the back of a former hardware store. The ceiling still had the hooks where somebody had once displayed lengths of chain. Her landlord wanted to remove them. Ada said they were holding up perfectly good dust and could be left alone.

She made coffee that evening in a pot older than Pike's documented ownership history.

“You wanted it to be real,” she said.

“I wanted him to quit smiling at me.”

“Both things can happen.”

She set down two mugs and pulled the copy between us.

I had asked her once whether she believed any of the family stories people brought her. She said belief was a personal matter and cataloging required a pencil. I liked her considerably more after that.

Now she drew three columns on a fresh sheet.

What we had. What we had been told. What we needed to find out.

In the first column she wrote: **Modern printed rendering supplied by Vernon Pike.**

In the second: **Claimed ancient family manuscript.**

The third column took the rest of the page.

“You ever feel like you're building a fence around a tornado?” I asked.

“Regularly. You're drinking out of its mug.”

The rendering began with a boy, a grain store, and a woman whose name had been removed.

Not lost. Removed.

The sentence was explicit: *I leave no name for the keeper, because a name can be made a handle.*

I read it aloud twice.

The child was called Ilen. His father had died owing grain. The merchant who held the debt claimed the boy as payment. The unnamed keeper stood at the storehouse door and asked to see the record.

That was the beginning. No throne, no blood-red moon, no army coming over the ridge with a soundtrack. A woman asking a man to show his arithmetic.

“That's a dangerous woman,” I said.

Ada marked the page number.

On the next page the merchant's clerk had listed the father's debt, the charges added after his death, and the boy's supposed value. The keeper asked where the merchant had recorded the father's final delivery.

There was no answer.

Outside, a pickup rattled past the old storefront. Inside, the refrigerator clicked off. The quiet came close around the table.

My phone lit up.

**VERNON PIKE: Another buyer has expressed interest. I would hate to see your family's legacy leave your hands.**

I turned the screen toward Ada.

“Apparently my ancestors are on a timer.”

“Don't reply tonight.”

“I was going to ask whether they came with roadside assistance.”

“Especially that.”

She placed a clean sheet beneath the copy. We could examine the story without accepting the sales pitch. We could keep every page without buying the box. Those were ordinary choices. Pike had worked hard to make them feel unavailable.

“Somebody wrote this,” I said. “Somebody put my name on it. Pike is asking me to pay for a family connection he hasn't established. He owes us an answer.”

“Yes.”

“And somebody chose my name.”

“Yes.”

I picked up the pencil.

Under Ada's third column I wrote:

**Why me?**

## Chapter Three — The Keeper at the Door

*From the supplied rendering. Its purported ancient setting is part of the manuscript's story; its date and authorship remain unresolved within the investigation.*

The keeper arrived while the merchant was still explaining the goodness of his arrangement.

He had washed his hands. He had summoned witnesses. He had placed a bowl of clean water beside the door so that everyone entering could see how carefully he observed the customs of decent men.

The boy stood beside the grain scales.

“He will be fed,” the merchant said.

“Then feed him.”

“He will be housed.”

“Then house him.”

The merchant looked toward his witnesses, giving them time to appreciate how unreasonable she was being.

“There is a debt.”

“You have mentioned it. Show it.”

The clerk brought the record. He was a narrow young man who held his writing hand against his stomach when he was not using it. The keeper had seen men carry injured birds that way.

She studied the marks.

“His father delivered grain at the end of the rains.”

“Against another obligation.”

“Where is that obligation?”

The merchant reached for the record. She did not surrender it.

Behind her, the boy shifted his weight. One of his sandals had broken at the heel. He kept his toes pressed downward to hold it on his foot.

“Ask him,” the merchant said. “The boy knows his father owed me.”

The keeper turned.

“Did your father bring grain?”

Ilen nodded.

“Were you there?”

“I carried the little measure.”

“Who received it?”

He looked at the clerk.

The clerk lowered his eyes.

The merchant laughed gently, as men sometimes do when they want cruelty to arrive dressed as patience.

“A child remembers what comforts him.”

The keeper put the record on the table.

“Then we shall ask an adult.”

Nobody moved.

She waited until the clerk looked up.

“Did you receive it?”

He glanced at the merchant. The room seemed to contract around that small motion.

“I received grain.”

“From this boy's father?”

“Yes.”

“Where did you enter it?”

The clerk's mouth opened. Before he could speak, the merchant struck the table with his palm.

“This is my house.”

The bowl of water jumped. A little spilled over its edge.

The keeper looked at the wet table, then at the boy.

“Ilen. Who is waiting for you?”

“My aunt.”

“Where?”

“By the lower well.”

One of the witnesses, a woman who had delivered empty sacks, stepped away from the wall.

“I know her.”

The keeper addressed the woman directly.

“Will you bring her?”

The merchant moved toward the doorway.

Now his witnesses had something to witness.

He stopped with one hand against the frame. The sack woman passed beneath his arm and went out.

The keeper pulled a stool away from the scales.

“Sit,” she told Ilen.

He looked at the merchant for permission.

The keeper saw him do it. So did the clerk.

She placed the stool beside her own feet and waited. At last the boy sat.

For the first time since she entered, the merchant had to look down to see the person he had been pricing.

## Chapter Four — The Boy Who Wasn't in the Sale

I shut the copy.

Ada let me sit with it.

There are people who can see you hit a hard place and immediately start explaining the hard place. Ada had the good sense to drink her coffee.

“He made the boy ask the buyer for permission to sit down,” I said.

“Yes.”

“And Pike made me ask him for permission to see my own supposed history.”

She pushed the notebook closer.

“Write down the resemblance. Don't let it answer the questions for you.”

I wrote it. Then I turned to the next page of the rendering.

It was missing.

We had pages one through twelve, then fourteen through nineteen. The number thirteen had not been covered or torn away. There simply wasn't a thirteenth sheet in the packet.

“An accident?” I asked.

“Ask for it.”

I sent Pike a short message. No roadside assistance. No family discount. Just the page number and the request.

He replied within a minute.

**That portion is restricted to the purchaser.**

I read the sentence aloud.

“Well, damn. He's put the ancestors behind a paywall.”

Ada took a photograph of the message with its time visible.

The following morning we returned to the shop. Pike wasn't there. A young man stood behind the counter labeling a tray of spoons.

He had ink on the side of one finger and a piece of bread on a paper towel beside the till.

“Mr. Pike will be in after lunch,” he said.

“We need a missing page from the copy he supplied.”

The young man's hand stopped over the spoons.

“Which copy?”

I put the cover on the counter. My name, large and handsome, sat beneath the title.

He stared at it a little longer than he needed to.

“I can't give out files.”

“I'm not asking you to,” Ada said. “Can you leave him a message?”

“Yes.”

He reached for a pad.

“Your name?”

I tapped the cover.

A smile started at the corner of his mouth. Then his eyes went to the camera above the register, and the smile stopped.

“Right.”

He wrote the message.

On our way out, I noticed a wastebasket full of strips of trimmed paper. One strip showed the edge of a thirteen-point seal.

I pointed it out to Ada once we were outside.

“Those trimmings establish that printed copies are being made here,” she said. “They don't establish who made the original leaf. Pike still owes us its source.”

“Then we ask for the source. He doesn't get to sell the gap as proof.”

“We saw paper trimmings. That's what we saw.”

“You ever get tired of doing that?”

“Yes.”

She unlocked the car.

“It's less tiring than repairing what happens when I don't.”

We were halfway back to her workroom when my phone rang from an unfamiliar number.

The young man spoke so quietly I pulled over to hear him.

“I'm not giving you anything from the shop.”

“All right.”

“I want that understood.”

“It is.”

“But you shouldn't pay him until you ask where he got your name.”

I looked at Ada.

She was already opening her notebook.

“What do I call you?” I asked.

“Caleb.”

“Caleb, are you somewhere you can talk safely?”

He took a breath.

“Not for long.”

“Then we don't do this now. Tell me a time that works.”

For several seconds there was only traffic at his end.

Then he said, “Thank you.”

## Chapter Five — A Handle on a Name

Caleb chose a diner beside a grocery store. He brought his aunt, who introduced herself as Della and asked the waitress whether the cornbread had sugar in it.

“Yes, ma'am.”

“Then I'll have toast.”

I liked her before we reached the business part.

Caleb was nineteen. Pike employed him three afternoons a week to scan photographs, crop images, and prepare sales sheets. He had permission to discuss his own work, Della said, but he had not taken property, copied customer files, or come to exchange anybody's private business for money.

“Nobody here asked him to,” Ada said.

“I'm making sure we remain nobody.”

“Fair enough,” I said.

Caleb unfolded a receipt from his pocket. It listed his hours and a job description: **Heritage presentation — personalized cover.**

He had kept it because Pike still owed him for that afternoon.

“The inside pages came as a scan,” Caleb said. “The cover was a template. He told me to put your name in the space.”

“Did you change anything inside?” Ada asked.

“No.”

“Did you make the old-looking leaf?”

“No. I never handled it.”

“Do you know how old it is?”

“No.”

Ada wrote each answer without rewarding the useful ones and frowning at the others. Caleb began to sit a little straighter.

“I saw your name in a list he made,” he said to me. “People asking about family history. Places they'd mentioned. Words they used.”

“Do you have that list?”

“No.”

“Then we don't claim we do.”

He nodded.

I looked at the receipt. Pike had charged forty thousand dollars for an inheritance and had not yet paid the young man who typed the heir's name.

The waitress brought our food.

Della tasted her toast, apparently satisfied that nobody had attempted to turn it into cake.

“He wants to leave the job,” she said. “He doesn't want a grand crusade. He wants his wages and another job.”

“Then that is what we talk about,” I said.

Caleb looked relieved enough to make me angry. Not at him.

A person ought to be able to tell you one true thing without getting drafted into the rest of your life.

Ada asked whether she could keep a photograph of his receipt with his address covered. He said yes. She asked whether his name could appear in anything public. He said no.

She wrote that down too.

After they left, I sat staring at the ring my glass had made on the table.

I had wanted an old story to open a door. Here was a living person standing in the opening, and I had almost started asking what else he could fetch for me.

“You caught yourself,” Ada said.

“After his aunt caught the room.”

“Good room to have an aunt in.”

Back at the workroom, I drew three headings in my notebook.

**Who acted?**

**Who benefited?**

**Who was harmed?**

I put Pike under the first heading. His proposed sale under the second. Under the third, I began with Caleb's unpaid hours.

Then I added myself under the first heading.

Ada saw it. She didn't say a word.

## Chapter Six — The Little Measure

*The narrative in the supplied rendering resumes. The missing thirteenth page remains absent from the modern copy.*

By the time Ilen's aunt reached the grain store, four more people had stopped in the doorway.

The merchant complained about the crowd. He had invited witnesses when he expected agreement. Their number became offensive once they began paying attention.

The aunt crossed the room without looking at him. She put one hand on the boy's shoulder and bent to hear what he said.

The keeper moved aside so they could speak.

“He is not yours to take,” the merchant said.

The aunt stood upright.

“He's not yours to keep.”

The clerk stared at the table. A drop from the overturned water bowl had reached his sleeve.

“There was another record,” he said.

The merchant turned.

The clerk's voice grew smaller, but it continued.

“For the delivery.”

“An accounting error.”

The keeper waited.

“You were told to correct it,” the merchant said.

“I was told to remove it.”

Nobody at the door spoke. The boy's aunt tightened her hand on his shoulder.

The clerk walked to the shelf beside the wall. Behind a row of empty containers stood a basket of broken records. He began searching it.

The merchant told him to stop.

He kept searching.

The keeper could hear the pieces knock together. She could hear the boy breathing. She could hear, outside, the ordinary business of the settlement continuing as though this room did not exist.

At last the clerk lifted two fragments.

They did not make a whole record. They preserved the father's mark and a quantity.

The keeper laid them beside the debt account.

The boy leaned forward.

“That's the little measure,” he said.

The clerk nodded.

“And the larger delivery?” the keeper asked.

“Recorded separately.”

“Can you find it?”

“I can look.”

The merchant came around the table. He was no longer smiling.

“Leave the boy. Take what the father owed and be done with it.”

The aunt stared at him.

“You would have me buy him?”

“I would have this disturbance finished.”

The keeper looked at the witnesses. One of them had been nodding, grateful for an arrangement that would let everybody go home without choosing a side.

“Listen to the arrangement,” she said. “He leaves with the child. The merchant keeps the payment. The false record remains. Tomorrow another child stands beside the scales.”

The nodding man became very interested in the floor.

The clerk found the third fragment.

It recorded the larger delivery. Together, the amounts exceeded the original debt. The merchant began explaining added charges.

The keeper asked when those charges had been agreed.

He began explaining the expense of storage.

She asked who had stored the grain.

He began explaining how much trouble he had taken for the father.

She looked at the fragments on the table.

“You have collected his grain,” she said. “You have erased his payment. Now you are asking for his son.”

The aunt took Ilen's hand.

The merchant moved toward them, and the sack woman stepped into the doorway. She did not threaten him. She called his neighbors by name.

One answered. Then another.

The merchant stopped.

Ilen and his aunt went out together. The keeper waited until they had crossed the open ground and reached the shelter beside the well.

Only then did she turn back to the clerk.

“You need to come too.”

He looked at the broken records.

“He will say I did this.”

“What did you do?”

“I removed the entry.”

She let the answer stand. The young man had removed a payment and helped put a child beside the scales. Speaking now did not erase that act. His next duty was to restore the entry, identify the instruction he had followed, and give the witnesses an accurate account.

“Bring what you've found,” she said. “We will record what you removed, who ordered it, and what you have restored. Your correction belongs in the record beside your part in the harm.”

“And my name?”

She looked toward the neighbors at the door.

“We will ask you where it can be kept.”

---

The next paragraph in my copy began in the middle of a sentence.

Page fourteen.

I turned back to twelve. Then forward again.

The child was out. The clerk had spoken. Whatever happened to the original records, whatever agreement the witnesses made, whatever name the keeper permitted anyone to write—Pike had withheld it.

On page fourteen, someone had drawn the thirteen-point seal.

Under it were three short lines. The supplied English rendering gave them as the questions I had already written in my notebook.

Who acted. Who benefited. Who was harmed.

I called Pike.

“I'd like the missing page.”

“Have you decided to proceed?”

“I've decided to ask for the missing page.”

“Nathan, I think we understand each other.”

“Then this ought to go quick.”

He sighed. It was a costly sigh, full of all the patience he believed he had spent on a difficult customer.

“The complete material transfers with the purchase.”

“Your cover was personalized last week. The name underneath the title is mine because somebody typed it there. You haven't shown me an older connection.”

Silence.

“You've spoken to my employee.”

“I've asked you about your evidence.”

“You're risking a remarkable opportunity.”

“Yes or no, Mr. Pike. Will you supply the page without a purchase?”

“No.”

“There we are. A whole answer. Thank you.”

I ended the call before he could dress it back up.

Ada stood at the worktable with an enlargement of the seal. Beside it lay a folder of printer's marks she had been assembling from her own reference collection.

“Come look,” she said.

The seal in Pike's rendering had thirteen points. One point bent inward. At first glance the bend resembled damage, a flaw in the scan or a stroke that had missed its place.

Ada pointed to a small printed emblem in the reference folder.

Thirteen points.

The same bent tip.

“How old?” I asked.

“The catalog dates this printed example to the seventeenth century. We still need the original item checked.”

I bent over both images.

There was another detail: a short cross-stroke near the center, easy to miss until you knew to look. It appeared in both.

Pike had offered me a straight road back beyond recorded names. Now the first signpost was pointing a different way.

“Same bent point. Same cross-stroke,” I said. “Now we trace who copied what.”

“First we inspect the printed example and the leaf,” Ada said. “Then we compare them with Pike's rendering. The images match; the direction of copying isn't established. That's the question he must answer before he gets to sell a lineage.”

I looked at the English words beneath the seal. OUR WORD IS OUR BOND. Somebody had known exactly where to put them.

“Well,” I said, pulling up a chair, “let's find out whose word we've been reading.”

Ada turned over the reference card.

On its back was the title of the volume that had carried the printer's mark.

**THE CARAVAN'S MIRROR.**
