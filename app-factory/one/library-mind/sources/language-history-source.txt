There are approximately 7,000 to 7,170 living languages spoken in the world today.
The exact total is constantly shifting as linguistic research catalogs undocumented tongues, and others unfortunately go extinct. The most definitive tracking is managed by Ethnologue, a leading research center for language intelligence, which currently tracks around 7,170 distinct languages.

Key Facts About World Languages

• Geographic Distribution: Language diversity is not spread evenly. Asia leads the world with roughly 2,300 languages, followed closely by Africa with over 2,100. By contrast, the entire continent of Europe has the fewest, with only about 287 languages.
• The Linguistic Super-Power: Papua New Guinea is the most linguistically diverse country on Earth, housing more than 830 different languages despite its small population.
• Unequal Speaker Distribution: A very small number of languages dominate global communication. Just 20 languages (a mere 0.3% of the world's total) are spoken as native tongues by over 3.7 billion people—more than half the global population.
• Endangered Status: Roughly 44% of all languages are endangered. These languages are spoken by small, aging communities of fewer than 1,000 users, leaving them at high risk of disappearing within the next century.

and languages that are no longer used and lost

Throughout human history, an estimated 25,000 distinct languages have gone completely extinct.
Linguists separate these non-living languages into two primary categories: dead languages (which have no native speakers but are still studied or used for specific rituals, like Latin) and extinct/lost languages (which have no speakers, are no longer studied, and in many cases, have vanished without leaving any written record).

The Scale of Lost Languages

• The Modern Toll: Documenting exact numbers is difficult because thousands of languages disappeared before writing was invented. However, data catalogs confirm that at least 573 languages have gone extinct in modern history (mostly since 1950), with over 400 lost in just the last 50 years.
• The Ancient Peak: Recent linguistic mapping suggests that global language diversity peaked between 1,000 and 3,000 years ago, when tens of thousands of languages were spoken simultaneously. The rise of sweeping historical empires (such as the Roman, Han, and British Empires) forced assimilation and triggered a massive collapse in linguistic variety.
• Entire Families Erased: We haven't just lost individual languages; we have lost entire ancestral branches. Historians have tracked roughly 100 entire language families that have completely died out over time.

Why Do Languages Die?

Languages are rarely dropped by choice. Instead, they disappear due to specific structural pressures:
1. Language Shift: Driven by globalization, younger generations are often forced or incentivized to learn a dominant national language (like English, Spanish, or Mandarin) for school and jobs, gradually abandoning their ancestral tongue.
2. Historical Trauma & Colonialism: Colonial policies, such as the forced placement of Indigenous children into boarding schools, systematically banned and broke the transmission of native languages.
3. The "One Every Fortnight" Rate: Because many remaining languages are spoken only by small groups of elders, the world is currently losing an active language every 14 days (about 25 to 30 languages a year).

The "Sleeping" Exception

Not all lost languages stay gone forever. Linguists now use the term "sleeping languages" to describe those with no living speakers but rich historical documentation. In remarkable cases like Hebrew, or ongoing community efforts for languages like Cornish, communities have successfully used old texts and audio recordings to wake the language up and raise a new generation of native speakers.

langauges that are reconzied as languagues - elf and star trek etc

Languages created for movies, books, and TV shows are known as constructed languages (or "conlangs").
While they started as fictional concepts, several have become officially recognized as real, functional languages by international linguistic bodies. They are not just collections of cool-sounding gibberish; they possess their own complete grammatical rules, full vocabularies, and structured syntaxes.
The most advanced conlangs are even granted an official ISO 639-3 language code—the exact same international standard registration used to classify Spanish, English, or Hindi.

The Most Recognized Fictional Languages

• Klingon (Star Trek): Created by linguist Marc Okrand for Paramount Pictures, Klingon is the most fully developed fictional language on Earth. It is officially recognized by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO code: tlh). It boasts an official Klingon Language Institute, translations of Shakespeare's Hamlet, an active Duolingo course, and hundreds of fluent speakers worldwide.
• Elvish (The Lord of the Rings): J.R.R. Tolkien was a professional philologist (language scientist) who famously admitted he built his fictional world around the languages he invented. His two primary Elvish tongues—Quenya and Sindarin—are deeply complex, featuring fully fleshed-out historical etymologies, distinct dialects, and fluid grammatical rules.
• Dothraki and High Valyrian (Game of Thrones): Author George R.R. Martin only wrote a few words of these languages in his books. However, for the HBO TV adaptation, linguist David J. Peterson was hired to build them out into real languages. Today, High Valyrian (ISO code: qvy) has thousands of words, complex noun cases, and can be actively learned online.
• Na'vi (Avatar): Director James Cameron wanted a distinct, printable language for his alien species. Linguist Paul Frommer created a complete phonetic structure and grammar system that includes unique rules, such as verbs that change meaning depending on the emotional state of the speaker.

How Fictional Languages Become "Real"

For a fictional language to graduate from a prop to a recognized tongue, it must pass three main linguistic thresholds:
1. Phonology: It must have a fixed system of sounds (phonemes) that speakers must strictly adhere to.
2. Morphology and Syntax: It needs structural rules governing how words are formed and how sentences are built (e.g., Klingon uses an uncommon Object-Verb-Subject sentence structure to make it sound intentionally alien).
3. A Living Community: The ultimate test of a language is whether people use it to communicate. Because fans actively write poetry, have conversations, and sing songs in tongues like Klingon and Elvish, linguists categorize them as living, constructed social languages.

the ancient versions of those

Both J.R.R. Tolkien and Marc Okrand treated their fictional worlds as living histories. Because of this, both Elvish and Klingon have intentional, fully realized "ancient" ancestral versions designed directly into their lore.

The Ancient Versions of Elvish

Tolkien did not just build a language; he simulated thousands of years of linguistic evolution. In his mythology, all Elvish tongues evolve from a singular ancient source:
• Primitive Quendian: This is the ultimate "Proto-Elvish" tongue. It was spoken by the very first Elves when they woke up under the stars at Cuiviénen. From this ancient root, the language fractured as different Elven tribes migrated across Middle-earth.
• Common Eldarin: The immediate descendant of Primitive Quendian, spoken during the great march of the Elves toward the West.
• The "Latin" of Middle-earth (Quenya): When you hear Elvish in the movies, you are usually hearing Sindarin (the common spoken tongue). Quenya is the ancient, high-formal version of Elvish. By the Third Age (the time of The Lord of the Rings), Quenya was no longer a daily spoken language; it was used exclusively as an "Elvish Latin" for ancient lore, poetry, and formal spells.
A classic example of how Tolkien evolved the language: The Primitive Quendian root word for "people" was Quendi. Over centuries of geographic separation, one migrating tribe naturally shifted the "Kw" sound to a "P", transforming the word into Pendi, while an isolated group drop the "W" entirely, shortening it to Ki.

The Ancient Versions of Klingon

In the Star Trek universe, the harsh, guttural language spoken by modern warriors is actually a heavily streamlined version of a much older tongue.
• no'Ich (Old Klingon): This is the canonical ancestor of modern Klingon. According to lore provided by creator Marc Okrand, no'Ich was an incredibly complex language spoken centuries ago.
• The Linguistic Great Awakening: Modern Klingon became the dominant tongue after a major language standardization effort on the homeworld of Qo'noS. As the Klingon Empire expanded, the warrior caste intentionally stripped out the highly poetic, complex noun-shuffling structures of no'Ich to favor short, punchy military commands.
• The "Ancient" Dialect: In Star Trek lore, certain remote regions of the Klingon homeworld (like the Morskan quadrant) still speak a dialect that preserves elements of this ancient grammatical structure, heavily utilizing prefix-dropping and different phonetic rolls that modern Klingons consider old-fashioned or backwater.

1. Living Languages (~7,170 entries)

This section forms the active, breathing baseline of your database.
• The Challenge: Capturing dialects and regional variations. A language like Arabic isn't just one language; Moroccan Arabic and Levantine Arabic are practically mutually unintelligible.
• Core Data Needed: ISO 639-3 codes, geographical coordinates, speaker counts, audio samples of native speech, and grammatical rules.
• Primary Sources to Integrate: Ethnologue and Glottolog.

2. Extinct & Lost Languages (~25,000 entries)

This is where the database becomes an archeological archive.
• The Challenge: Dealing with a total lack of data. Many of the 25,000 lost languages have no written records because they were strictly oral.
• The Tiers of Death: You will need to categorize these by Recorded Extinct (like Latin or Ancient Egyptian, where we have text), Unrecorded Extinct (known to have existed via historical accounts, but no text remains), and Proto-Languages (reconstructed ancestral tongues like Proto-Indo-European).
• Core Data Needed: Last known date of use, archaeological region, writing system (cuneiform, hieroglyphs, runic), and scanned historical manuscripts.

3. Fictional & Unrecognized Languages (Conlangs)

This section bridges the gap between pop culture, philosophy, and fringe linguistics.
• The Challenge: Defining what "counts" as a language. Anyone can sit down and make up a 10-word language for a school project. Your database will need a threshold for entry.
• Categories to Include:
	• Artistic Languages (Artlangs): Klingon, Elvish, Dothraki, Na'vi.
	• Auxiliary Languages (Auxlangs): Esperanto or Interlingua (built to be a real-world universal second language).
	• Engineered Languages (Englangs): Lojban (a language based entirely on strict mathematical logic).
• Core Data Needed: Creator name, date of creation, media source (book, TV, film), and community size.
