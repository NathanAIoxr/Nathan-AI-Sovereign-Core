# Sunday Buffet Slam-Down

## 11:55 AM Amen. 12:10 PM Ranch.

The last amen hangs above the pews. Somebody reaches for a coat. Somebody asks where everybody is going for lunch, even though everybody already knows. The preacher has been talking about appetite for forty-five minutes. Now he is talking about getting there before the other congregation.

At the restaurant, Table 15 has a stack of chairs around it and a clear aisle beside it. The server needs that aisle. Two hot plates are coming out of the kitchen and one of them has to go all the way to the back. Before she gets there, somebody at Table 15 snaps for a side of ranch.

HOLD EVERYTHING AND CLEAR THE AISLE. For once, that instruction is for the table. Let the woman get through with the food she already has in her hands.

This is a fictional Sunday at a Southern buffet. The preacher and his wife are invented Independent Fundamental Baptist (IFB) characters. The clock, the portions, the number on the check and the dialogue belong to this story. Restaurant workers’ accounts of similar encounters are listed in the source notes.

## Two plates apiece

The preacher comes back from the buffet with fried chicken in the lead. A serving of roast beef runs along the rim, two scoops of potatoes meet in the middle, and he draws a stripe of brown gravy through them. The mac goes in wherever there is room. He picks up two yeast rolls to catch what the fork leaves behind. On his second trip he takes another piece of chicken, then heads to the dessert counter for banana pudding and peach cobbler. The sweet tea gets one refill.

His wife works the other way around the line. Roast beef gets the first space on her plate. Chicken, potatoes, gravy, mac and a roll follow. On her second trip she goes back for a second helping of roast beef and a second scoop of mac. Then she gets the banana pudding and cobbler she had already decided on. She has one glass of sweet tea. She makes both trips herself and orders for herself when the server asks.

A rack of empty plates is three steps from the line. A stack of clean glasses is behind the server station. Neither diner sees the hands that keep those stacks full. They both notice when the bread basket runs low.

The joke is that the sermon had room for everybody else's appetite. At the buffet, his own appetite gets two plates and a refill. When the two minutes for cornbread are up, the kitchen has done exactly what it said it would do.

## How much did they eat?

In the Sunday spread, the preacher's first plate comes to 1,305 calories. The second plate comes to 810. Two 12-ounce sweet teas add 300. His example total is 2,415 calories. His wife's plates come to 1,050 and 920. Her one tea adds 150. Her example total is 2,120. Together the two diners come to 4,535 calories.

Here is the arithmetic behind the plates. One piece of fried chicken is set at 320 calories. Three ounces of roast beef are 210. A half cup of mashed potatoes is 140 and a quarter cup of gravy adds 45. A half cup of mac is 220 and a yeast roll is 115. A half cup of banana pudding is 240, a half cup of peach cobbler is 250, and a glass of sweet tea is 150. ## The work between the bites

At 12:10, the ranch can wait the length of a walk down the aisle. The server sets down two hot plates, takes a glass out of the way, answers a question about the sides, and turns around. Table 15 is still there. So is the ranch at the salad bar. If a guest gets it, the rest of the work happens in the order it was already happening.

At 12:24, the cornbread needs two minutes. A request for the manager sends the server to the kitchen, down the hall and back to the table. The cornbread still needs two minutes. When the next tray comes out, the cook is trying to keep the tray level on the way through an aisle people have crowded into from both sides.

At 12:52, the table is done eating. Somebody has ground crackers into the floor under a chair. Somebody has pushed a wad of napkins into a wet saucer. The host can see the next party watching. The server has to sanitize the table whichever way the guests leave it. A free aisle and a pile of dry trash she can pick up in one reach change the time that takes.

The tip has its own line in dollars. You can make one good decision and one rotten one before the same plate comes back empty.

## The folded bill

At 1:00 the check says $48.20 before tax. A guest puts folded paper under the edge of a plate. A corner looks like cash. The server puts a hand on the plate, takes the paper and opens it. It is a tract. The till reads the same before and after she opens it. Zero dollars went from the table to the tip line.

Run the same check with a 10% tip and $4.82 is paid. At 20%, $9.64 is paid and the example total comes to $57.84 before tax. Put the bill and the tip on separate lines. Anybody at the table can verify them.

The U.S. Department of Labor says the federal rule can permit a $2.13 hourly direct cash wage when a covered employer lawfully takes a tip credit. The employer has to make up a shortfall if qualifying tips and direct wages fail to meet the applicable floor over the workweek. State rules can provide higher protections. The tip on one $48.20 restaurant check and the employer's wage obligation are different entries.

The preacher can finish his prayer. The guest can take the tract home, hand it over openly, or leave it beside a real tip. The piece of paper still has a cash value of zero. The server's shift is still work.

## Who you are, whom you be, and what you bee

WHO YOU ARE. Nathan Tabor puts it from the preacher's-kid side of the table. Owens Cross Roads, Alabama. Youngest of three boys. Hooked on Phonics, McGuffey Readers, the King James Bible, television, movies and music. Grandmother Opal Phillips Tabor's instruction, as he recalls it, was “Play dumb until you know what you're dealing with.”

WHOM YOU BE. “Well, whatca need done—and when you need it done by?” That is the question he wants asked before anybody starts talking about how important they are. He describes his rule as sharing 20% right off the top. In this story that rule gets one ordinary test. How many dollars did the worker receive when the check closed?

WHAT YOU BEE. The honey and the stinger. Mercy for the person doing the work and a sharp tongue for somebody who expects the sermon to excuse the way they treated her. If a table asks for grace over lunch, the person bringing lunch should get some of it.

## Pulpit, plate and proof

Proverbs 23:20–21 (KJV) puts gluttony in the sermon. James 5:4 (KJV) puts the laborer's wages in the sermon. At Table 15 those passages have details somebody can read off the receipt. There is how much each person takes, how much extra work they ask for, and what actually changes hands.

The rush winds down. The host picks up the stack of menus. The server wipes a last streak of gravy off Table 15. Up front the preacher is holding the door while his wife checks that she has the car keys. Next Sunday another group will walk in the same door. Its choices start at the host stand, long before the tip line.
