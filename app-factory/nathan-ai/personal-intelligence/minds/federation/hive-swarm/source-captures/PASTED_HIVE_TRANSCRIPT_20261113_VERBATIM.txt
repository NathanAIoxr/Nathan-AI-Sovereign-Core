The exact number fluctuates dramatically throughout the year as the colony adapts to changing weather and nectar availability.

Population by Season

Honey bee colonies expand and contract naturally to survive the year.
• Peak Summer. A thriving colony typically reaches 40,000 to 60,000 bees, though exceptionally strong hives can climb up to 80,000.
• Winter. The colony shrinks to roughly 8,000 to 20,000 bees. They form a tight cluster inside the hive to conserve heat and survive on stored honey.
• Spring. As flowers bloom, the queen ramps up egg laying to rebuild the population.
• New Hives. A starter colony, often bought as a 3-pound package of bees, begins with about 10,000 bees.

Breakdown of the Colony

Every bee in the hive has a specific role based on its caste.
• One Queen. She is the only reproductive female and lays up to 1,500 to 2,000 eggs per day during the busy summer months.
• Thousands of Workers. Female worker bees make up over 98% of the hive. They clean the hive, feed larvae, build wax comb, guard the entrance, and forage for nectar and pollen.
• Hundreds of Drones. Male bees appear mostly in the spring and summer. Their sole purpose is to mate with a virgin queen, and they are evicted from the hive before winter to save food.
You can read more about colony cycles and hive structure from the British Beekeepers Association and Nettie's Bees.
