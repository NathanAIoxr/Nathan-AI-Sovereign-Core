# Book 1 | The Ford Stone

The day Pee Tabor put the ford stone in backward, six people came down to the water to tell him about it. Three of them were related to him. One of them had never set a stone in his life. All six were right.

“The notch goes upstream,” Dee said.

“You might have mentioned that before I got it in.”

“I did. You said you could hear better with your mouth open.”

At fifteen, Pee was old enough to be sent across the river with a message and young enough to be told exactly how many steps to take once he got there. His cousin was a year younger and had never accepted either restriction. She stood knee-deep beside him with a length of cord in one hand, measuring the water against an old scratch in the bank. He liked to say there were things she would measure twice that he could fix once. More often than he cared to admit, fixing them once meant waiting for her to measure them twice.

The old ford stone had shifted after the last flood. Their grandmother Aster had set it when the two of them were small. The notch marked the depth above which the walking ford became a dangerous proposition, even for somebody who knew the bottom. To leave it wrong was to tell a stranger that the river was safe when it might be rising.

Aster lowered herself onto a root. “Who’s coming from the west bank today?”

“The clay boats,” Pee said. He’d promised Sena, who worked the good clay on that side, that he would haul a new basket of it up to the firing shelter before supper. The valley’s finest bowls were Sena’s. Her worst ones were his, because she made him keep everything he spoiled.

“Who else?”

“Whoever needs the ford,” Dee said.

They lifted the stone again.

By late afternoon the first clay boat had come, with Sena’s niece Nella in the bow carrying a sealed pot in her lap. She held it up to show them without letting anyone see what was inside. The adults shifted their baskets to make a place for her. Even Aster asked permission. Nella looked pleased and said no to every single one.

Then an upstream branch bumped against the stone and spun. Dee watched it pass once more. It was a freshly broken branch. After it came a little island of grass with its roots still on. She looked at the notch she and Pee had just turned upstream. Water lapped over it.

“The second boat’s at the old landing,” said Sena. “They’ll think they can walk across from there.”

The shortest path ran straight down a steep cut that would fill before the ford did. Pee took the high bank. He climbed through briars and gave the boat crew the first signal that came to him, a long yell that sounded so much like somebody falling that they nearly jumped in to save him. Dee reached a bend beneath him and used the hand signal Sena had taught them. The men stopped. One turned a boat pole across the bow so the other would have something to hang on to when the current hit. Both came home by the upper trail. Some of that year’s clay went downriver.

After dark the valley ate at Sena’s shelter. Nella finally lifted the lid of her pot. Inside was a clay fish with six fins and teeth like the points of thorns.

“It’s for the ford,” she said. “So the real fish will know which way to go.”

Pee had three explanations ready for everything wrong with that. Nella looked at him the way Dee looked at him when his mouth was about to open. He took the fish down to the bank and set it high under a root, where she had a chance of finding it again.

The next morning the river was back below the notch. Pee put his hand on the stone he and Dee had set. He’d repaired dozens of things his family used. This was the first thing he had ever repaired for people whose names he would never know. He pushed it once more to make sure it held.

# Book 2 | The Seed House

The west-bank families arrived with empty baskets and found the high-bank families sitting on the seed-house steps. Two of the men were eating roast squash right where everyone could see them. If either man hoped that would make the discussion easier, he’d misunderstood the nature of hunger.

A late frost had killed the low fields. The people on the high ground had done better. Somebody had cut the cord across the door of the common storehouse. Aster’s records showed eleven baskets of planting corn. There were seven inside. People who had thought they could put up with a thin winter because spring would bring something better started counting what they could lose between then and planting.

“Ask who went in,” said Bran, the keeper of the high fields.

“Ask who was owed,” said Mara, who dressed wounds and had never in her life mistaken a loud voice for a good answer.

Pee went round the back to look for tracks. He found one broad bare foot pressed into the clay. Beside it was a mark where somebody had drawn a stick. He knew those tracks. Orin had broken his leg in the autumn and still walked with a stick on cold mornings.

Dee found Orin mending a basket with the rim tucked between his knees. She sat beside him while he tried three times to pass a strip of bark through the same hole. He asked her if they meant to make him give back what he’d eaten.

“How much did you eat?”

“A handful. Then my sister had some. Then her baby chewed on one grain until he fell asleep. You can put down all three names.”

Pee arrived expecting to catch Orin in a lie. Orin showed him the corner where he’d left two full baskets after taking them from the house in the dark. The roof above that corner had leaked through a crack. By morning the kernels were wet and starting to heat. Orin had been carrying the good corn away from the drip. He had eaten from a discarded basket of broken kernels while he worked. Pee tipped a few into his palm. The seedlings inside had begun to push through.

“Who told you to move it?”

“Nobody. I could hear the drip from my bed.”

That accounted for two baskets. It took the rest of the afternoon to account for the other two. Bran had borrowed them before the frost, when he thought everybody would have crops to spare. He had told the person at the door to count them as if they were still there. The person at the door had been his daughter.

They met beside the storehouse at dusk. Dee expected Bran to say that cutting the cord was worse. Instead he laid his own unopened planting basket on the steps and asked what was left of the wet corn. His daughter took a seat beside Orin. She was the first one to ask what his sister needed.

Aster had Dee put two marks beside Bran’s name, one for what he had taken and one for what he had brought back. She had her write down the best place to spread wet grain if a roof leaked again. Mara brought pots from three kitchens and had the children separate sound kernels from mildewed ones under her eye. Supper took longer that night. Pee spent part of it wishing somebody would thank him for finding the tracks. When he saw Orin balancing his good leg over a stick, he let it go and carried a pot to Orin’s sister.

In spring the families sowed a reserve field on ground high enough to miss the worst of the cold. On the first warm night Aster found Dee turning the storehouse record over in her hands.

“You forgot to put Orin down for what he ate,” Aster said.

Dee reached for the charcoal.

“Put him down for saving two baskets first.”

# Book 3 | The Tree That Lied

Sena’s trading party reached the hill with four bundles of reeds and one boy too young to keep anybody’s pace. The boy called himself Lark. He was from beyond the ridge, where people took a different trail to get almost everywhere and said some of their words with a rising sound Dee found pleasantly difficult to copy. He’d seen the forked branch tied to a sapling below the ridge and wanted to know whether it meant that good fishing lay beyond it.

“It means the ground is bad,” Pee said.

“How was I supposed to get that from a stick?”

By then Pee had lifted the boy bodily off a thin crust of earth hiding a deep washout. He was in no mood to admit that Lark had asked a sensible question.

That night Ivo, Sena’s older brother, went up to the hill to find out what had undermined the trail. He was home inside an hour wearing an expression that made Aster send the little ones away. From the bottom of the wash he had heard his daughter call him. His daughter had died before Nella was born, and she had used the particular name for him she’d had before she could say the adult one.

Aster waited until the next afternoon to bring Pee and Dee. The wash had opened beneath a tree that had grown out of a crack in a great flat stone. She showed the cousins a sound plank across the mouth of the wash. She told them to call to each other from opposite ends of it. In the hollow below, Pee heard his voice repeat the offer he’d made Lark. He listened for a second echo and heard his own mother say that it was time to come home. By the time she’d finished speaking, his left foot was over the edge.

Dee put her elbow through his. From the path she asked the hollow what her father had taken in his left hand the day he left on his last trip. The hollow had no answer. She had been too small to ask at the time, so no one had ever said the answer aloud near the place. She asked it three more questions that made no sound afterward at all. Then she asked it what Pee had said about the stick. It repeated him before she’d finished.

“Can it get out?” Pee asked.

The roots of the tree were pulling free of the bank. Aster gave him a long look and sent both cousins down for help.

They brought people from Sena’s party and from the two nearest clearings. Together they closed the old trail and laid a new one on firmer ground. Ivo proposed they cut down the tree and never hear it again. He was the one who changed his mind when he saw the stone start to roll as its roots shifted. They shored up the earth above it and settled the roots into place. At dusk Pee heard his mother again, faintly this time, and held the end of the cord while a woman he’d met that day packed down the last of the soil.

Dee argued for carving a warning. Lark asked her what good that would do anybody who hadn’t grown up with their marks. By morning they had laid a row of stones across the old approach and used a second row to lead people long enough to understand where the new trail went. At both ends, a pair of neighbors stayed to explain it until the next trading parties had come through.

Lark worked two days longer than he had promised. Just before he left, he made up an alarming story about Pee that would have been a lie if it hadn’t made Pee laugh. Dee watched the two of them trade food for their separate journeys. Beyond the ridge she could still see the little forked branch. She untied it and brought it home.

# Book 4 | The Winter Herd

Orin finished his first good winter boots two days before the buffalo came down to the meadow. He had spent a whole season working the hide and another morning cutting a seam out of one boot and sewing it again. When a younger child asked why the marks around the ankle curled in two different directions, Orin said it was an old design with a deep meaning. Dee said the deep meaning was that he’d started sewing facing the wrong end.

The buffalo passed below the frost line, where the wind had swept dry grass clear of the snow. Their backs looked like a second, slower hillside. Pee had seen them before from a long way off. He had never heard so many breathe at once.

Old Reeve took the cousins and Orin to watch the hunt. Pee had asked to join. Reeve gave him the pot of water and told him he could complain when he was good enough to go home carrying it full. They settled where they could see the lower grass. One adult animal was taken. The rest moved out in a surge so sudden the nearby birds rose all together. For a time Pee had the impression that the meadow itself had broken loose.

The work took the day. Hunters, hide workers and people from the nearest homes came down to share it. The children were sent uphill with the first food to get it into the cold. Mara stayed with a hunter who had turned an ankle and was furious that he might be carried home by his youngest brother. She asked how much he weighed. He told her. She told the brother to bring two more people. The hunter went on being furious all the way up, which struck Pee as a sign that he was going to be all right.

The dispute came when Bran’s brother proposed keeping the hide at the meadow. A trade party was due and the hide would fetch something that his household badly needed. Orin listened while older people argued. When somebody said his family had been given too much after the frost, he took off one new boot and put it on the table.

“Here,” he said. “I can start giving it back now.”

Nobody reached for it. People spent the next hour finding out what everybody thought everybody else had promised. Bran’s brother needed a good axe head. Orin’s sister needed something warm for her baby. One man could put up meat that other houses had no room to store. By the end they’d agreed to pool smaller hides for the axe head, and to ask the woman who knew the hottest part of the firing shelter if she’d make room for Orin’s sister and the baby. Orin took his boot back without comment.

The following morning he and Pee went out with Dee to bring water to the workers. A calf had strayed into a patch of low growth by the river and the herd was already moving beyond the bend. Pee began down toward it. Orin, who had been watching the bigger animals, said to wait. A cow wheeled out of the last group and came back at a run. They stepped behind the bank and gave her room. When the pair were out of sight, Orin got the younger two a drink from a skin he had carried inside his coat to keep it from freezing.

“You were going to pick it up, weren’t you?” he asked Pee.

“I was going to see if it needed picking up.”

Orin accepted this with a serious nod. On the way home he asked Dee to remember it exactly for the next time anyone said that about him.

That night Pee watched frost form on the inside of his window screen. He thought he could still hear the herd breathing. He put on his boots and went out to prove to himself that the sound was in his head. For several minutes he could hear it plainly from the rise above the field. He listened until it moved out of reach.

# Book 5 | The Two Landings

The day the east landing gave way, nobody was on it except Lark, which was unfortunate because he was there to tell people how badly it needed fixing. The lower boards went downstream. He grabbed an overhanging root and had time to remark that he’d expected something of the sort before Pee and Sena got him out.

By evening each bank had an explanation. The west-bank families said the eastern landing had been left to rot. The east-bank families said the western boats had battered it. The dead fish that turned up downstream the next morning gave both sides something new to blame on the other.

There was going to be a meeting. Pee expected to sit through it with his hands under his legs until someone gave him something to do. Instead Sena told him he and Dee would each spend an afternoon on the opposite bank and tell the meeting what they learned.

“Can we use a boat?” Pee asked.

“You can use your ears,” she said. “The rest is up to you.”

On the west bank Pee found a man scraping out a canoe on borrowed ground. The man’s family had moved their work to the high bank after the spring flood and now owed a share of the season’s catch for the space. The water at the old fishing place had gone slack after the new landing posts were driven. There had been little to catch.

On the east bank Dee found Nella turning fish over with a stick. A heron kept trying to steal the least spoiled ones. Nella paid more attention to it than to the argument, but she had been there when the landing was patched. She remembered the men filling an old side channel with brush to hold one of the outside posts.

Dee took Pee up to the side channel. Behind the brush the water was warm and still. Pee waded out until he could see the whole shape against the bank. The worst rot had started on the side no one could see from a boat. The two of them pulled a little brush free and lowered a clay dish to watch where the surface current took it. The dish slid toward the old landing and went straight under the hanging boards.

At the meeting each cousin had to speak where a relative could hear an uncomfortable answer. Pee told the west bank the boats had been forced through a crooked approach. Dee told the east bank whose patch had probably changed the flow. Neither expected the woman who had lent out her high ground to be the first one to change her mind. She asked for a new place to clean fish and offered to let the borrower work off his debt there, one morning at a time.

Sena drew two landing sites in damp earth with the blunt end of a reed. There would be work for both banks and two paths down instead of one. Each bank would lend labor to the other. The small catch would be shared until the fishing recovered. If it didn’t, they’d meet again.

On the morning the new landing opened, Lark took the cousins out in the first boat. He refused to get off until Pee jumped on the boards three times. Pee did it, sent spray into Lark’s face, and waited to find out what he would say.

“Feels safe now,” Lark told him. “I made you look foolish in front of a lot of people and it held.”

Before winter Dee and Lark carried one of the old boards up out of the flood line. On the back they scratched the two landings and the paths between them so runners could show visitors where to go. There was still room on the board when they finished. Dee thought somebody would put an arrow there one day for a road she hadn’t walked yet.

# Book 6 | The Names We Carry

Old Reeve died on the coldest morning of the year. Pee and Dee knew because Orin came to tell them with Reeve’s carrying strap over his shoulder and forgot to take it off when he sat down.

“I know he gave it to you,” Dee said.

“He gave it to me every time he had something heavy,” Orin said. “I don’t think that means it’s mine.”

Pee sat with him until the first people came up the path. He had been saving a particularly good stone for an afternoon when Reeve could show him how to see the best way to split it. There was a thing he had planned to ask on that afternoon about the winter hunt. All the important questions he had put off crowded in at once. When he looked over at Dee, she was holding the door for Mara and watching the floor.

At Reeve’s house they gave the people who came to sit with the family whatever food could be spared. Nella wandered among them gathering stories. She collected an account of a day Reeve had dropped a cooking pot on his bare foot and continued talking for twenty minutes. His daughter supplied the names of two places where he had set a shelter anyone could use. A woman from the next clearing remembered him carrying her father home after a fall. The daughter wanted that part included.

While they worked, Dee asked an old woman who had come from her father’s bank what he had carried in his left hand on the day he disappeared.

“A spare sandal,” the woman said. “The one on his foot was coming apart.”

Dee had imagined a knife or a beautiful stone. She asked the woman to say it again. The woman did, and told her that his other sandal had always been too big. It was the first new thing Dee had learned about him in years.

Aster spread the seed commitments and route records across her mat. The valley’s keepers used their own marks for households and things owed. A mark could lead Dee to a name if she’d learned it from the person who used it. Looking at it alone would never have told her about a man’s sandals. Aster gave her granddaughter an empty strip of bark to add to the packet.

“Keep the places where the winters go hard,” she told Dee. “The names you carry in your head you can pass on over supper.”

The cousins went to the ford the next day. On a visit there as little children, they had pretended the two creeks feeding the river were theirs. Pee claimed the loud one. Dee chose the smaller one because, when the valley went dry, it never quit.

He put Reeve’s strap on the flat top of the ford stone. Dee asked if it belonged there. He said he’d wanted to see what it looked like outside his house. They stood a little while and then carried it to Orin.

By the time evening came, the cousins had learned most of the route from their bank to the west field by heart, along with a second route that ran through Lark’s country and a third that cut uphill past the washout. Their job was to pass those ways on before the next wet season. Pee stood at his door after Dee had gone home and named the people he would tell first. He could walk to any one of their houses in the dark.

# Book 7 | The Man Without a Reflection

## The Rest of the Morning

Nella had won the right to choose the first game that morning by speaking up before either of the cousins got a word out. She took them down to the rocks and announced that the game was finding out how many different sounds the river could make before you got hungry.

“You win already,” Pee said. “I’m hungry.”

The three of them traded places on the rocks until they had a flat sound, a sound that went *chup*, and one deep inside a crevice that Nella was waiting to hear a second time. She took her little clay fish out from under its root. Its tail had broken off when the river rose, and she was taking it to her aunt to see if it could be mended.

With the girl safely up the path, the cousins brought fish traps in from the shallows. Aster wanted one of them home to carry bundles from the seed house. They’d had two good harvests since the frost and the bundles had grown heavy enough that Pee could hardly wait to show Orin he could manage them himself. He and Dee sat awhile to eat. They would have time to finish their meal if they went up the near path.

A potter on the far bank called across to ask whether their grandmother wanted another cooking pot. Her old one leaked. Dee called back that she would say yes as long as it didn’t cost anything. The potter promised she’d describe it as a birthday gift this time and ask about payment in the spring. They were still laughing when she went on up the hill.

Dee thought she might ask Aster for the little book of household marks that evening. She wanted to learn to make a copy on good bark from the spare stack at Sena’s. The creek had been high two years running, and one book in one house was too easy to lose. She was choosing which households she would start with when Pee held up the last fish in his basket. It was a small fish, and she gave him enough trouble about it that he carried it along as evidence.

At the last turn she stopped walking. He went on for several steps before he noticed she’d stopped making fun of him.

## The Far Bank

“Put it back,” Dee said.

“The fish?”

“Look at the river.”

Pee looked. The sycamores hung upside down in the water. So did the clouds. So did the black horse on the far bank, right down to the burrs in its tail.

The man holding its reins had no reflection at all.

Traders had told stories of white men with iron pots, and of lines drawn on paper that could make men with guns start walking. This was the first white man either cousin had ever seen. He took off his hat.

“Who owns this ford?”

“People on both sides use it,” Pee called.

The man set a ledger on a flat stone. He spoke enough of their language to ask his question. He understood the answer well enough to change it.

“Then no one owns it.”

When the pen touched the page, Aster’s ford stone split. One half sank where Pee couldn’t reach it, taking the notch with it.

“Write my name,” Dee said. “Write down that I’m here and I heard you.”

The stranger turned the ledger toward them. Across his writing he had drawn the mark the cousins used for an empty house. Under it were two smaller marks, exact copies of hers and Pee’s. Dee had never shown those marks to someone from beyond the ridge.

The cousins ran home. Aster heard them out while she shelled beans. When Pee finished, she got the book from its shelf and asked Dee to read the first name. In the place where its mark had been there was bare bark. Pee had eaten at that woman’s house the night before. He could get only the first sound of her name out of his mouth.

“Go to her house,” Aster said. “Ask her to tell you herself. Then ask who her mother was.”

Outside, a bell rang from the ford. There had never been a bell at the ford. Pee went to fetch the woman while Dee took the ridge path. From above she saw that the man was on their bank now, and two riders had joined him. One had a bell and the other a Bible. The first man took the Bible, read aloud about Cain and the mark that kept anyone from killing him, and slipped it into the same case as the ledger.

Dee waited for him to say something about Abel.

He lifted his head and called for the first household to come down to the ford.

# Book 8 | The People in the Margin

Pee found the woman whose name he had lost standing on her roof, throwing bundles through the smoke hole. He had expected to find her frightened. She was angry because the man at her door had been given the right to inventory her house and had begun with the bed her husband had made. She thought she might be permitted to keep it if she gave him enough other things to count.

“My name is Rowan,” she said when Pee finally asked. “My mother was Dell. You ever see your grandmother peel fruit down to the stone in one turn? Dell could do it with her eyes closed.”

The name came back so quickly Pee bit his cheek saying it. He looked down and found he could remember the woman’s kitchen again, right down to the crooked corner of her hearth. Rowan heard the man at the door close a chest and told Pee to stay on the ladder. From the roof she told the clerk that he was standing in her house, and asked who had sent him.

“The man who made the survey,” the clerk said.

“Then take him the news. Somebody lives here.”

By the time Pee got back to Dee, each family at the ford had been offered a slip of paper. The surveyor promised that marked households would be recognized and allowed to draw food from the common store. Everybody else would be trespassing. Dee watched an interpreter explain the offer twice to a man who could hear very little. The first time, she talked about the food. The second time, she talked about the signature. It was hard to tell from the surveyor’s smile whether he knew the difference mattered.

Bran wanted to take the slip for his own house. He was worried about the planting baskets. Dee asked him to let her see where his house was on the map before he accepted. He found it easily enough, but Sena’s shelter and the shared landings had been drawn with the same ink as his fence. The paper that promised to acknowledge his home gave him three other places that belonged to no one person. A separate line placed him in debt for every measure of corn he might take from them.

“I can keep the clerk out of my own kitchen,” he told Dee.

“Can you keep him out tomorrow if he says you owe him for today?”

Bran handed her the paper. She folded it twice to remember whose it was. While people still argued at the ford, the two cousins went to both landings and asked the workers who had built them to name what they’d contributed. Lark came down from the ridge. He remembered falling through the old landing in more detail than anyone needed, but he remembered whose boat had taken him home, too. Dee scratched the names into the underside of the route board. Everybody who wanted to could go there and see their own mark.

That evening the appointed clerk arrived to put a lock on the west landing. The new chain stopped short of its hook by two links. Pee knew because he was close enough to see the man stretch it until it cut his hand. He said nothing. Rowan said her name. Bran said whose boat he had borrowed to reach the landing. One by one the workers named the people who had helped build it. At last the clerk took his chain away.

It was nearly dark when news came that the men had reached the seed house. The families on the steps hadn’t all known the dead man whose mark had been copied onto the notice to sell it. The roof had been opened and the planting baskets carried down to a guarded wagon.

Pee wanted to go after it. Dee walked with him as far as the bend. They could see seven people they knew waiting by the ford to apply for food.

Rowan went down to stand with them. She had a bundle slung under her arm. She took out her supper, broke it in two and gave the larger half to the first person in line. By the time the clerk got to the door, the line faced the other way. People were asking Rowan who else might have something to spare.

# Book 9 | What the Paper Cost

Mara came to Dee with a slip of paper folded around a curl of hair. Orin’s sister had cut it from the boy after he went to sleep the previous night. By daylight the sleep place was bare. A wagon had taken him and three others downriver.

“The men say it was against a debt,” Mara told her. “They told his mother he’d be back after planting.”

Dee read the date and the marks underneath. It was a bill of sale.

Before the riders had come, Pee knew what was owed to whom from the way people talked over supper. He was beginning to see how a person might get into more trouble after the bill was paid than before it was written. Sena turned over one seller’s note and found the same seal she’d seen on a plate of meal charged to a hungry house. She thought the party would stop at the lower salt landing before daylight. Dee asked her whether she could still get there through the old side channel.

Sena tightened the strap on her bundle. “I can get there. Can you make yourself useful when we do?”

They found the salt landing in darkness and waited upstream for Lark. He arrived with the woman who ran the cookhouse there. Her sister, Kit, had been sold two winters ago and brought back once when the buyers thought she might be too ill to work. The cook had spent the days since then finding out whose names the men used when they sent papers back east.

She led them to a shed where the wagon driver was waiting for daylight. Pee asked if she could bring the children out with the morning workers. She said she could bring two in one trip under the eyes of the man who counted people passing the gate. All four were together inside. She thought they could get all four if they drew the man on duty far enough up the path.

“Why should we go?” asked one of the children when the cook told them what had been arranged. “They said my brother would be at the other end.”

The cook knelt down and gave him time to tell her his brother’s name. She said she would ask at the next landing. She told him what she knew of the place where the wagon was headed, and where the children would go instead if they left with her. He asked three more questions. When she went out, he took another child by the hand and went with her.

At dawn the cook took the two youngest children to the canoe. Lark walked the gate watchman up to the cookhouse to settle an argument about the salt bill. It was a real argument. Lark had just waited all evening to have it. The cook went back for the other two as soon as they were out of sight. She took the older child’s spare bundle to leave both his hands free.

Out on the landing Pee learned why Sena had brought a second canoe without telling him. Someone had set a watch on the main channel. Years before the party would have had to turn back there. The old branch of water the valley people had reopened ran into a low meadow. Sena took the first canoe through. A child who’d never been in a boat began to cry as soon as the bottom scraped the reeds. Pee told him how long his grandmother had made him stand in cold water to put the ford stone in right. The child asked why he told the story the way he did, with the part where he got it wrong at the beginning. Pee said it was the funniest part. After that they could both hear when the boat was scraping and when it simply sounded like it was.

Dee went back with the cook for the wagon papers. They copied the names and kept the bills where they found them. It would be hours before anyone knew precisely whose authority had been challenged. By sunrise the four children were in different boats headed to houses whose names weren’t on the survey map.

When Orin’s nephew reached his mother he put his hands in her hair. She touched the spot above his ear where she’d cut the curl and asked him about everything he’d eaten since he left. It was a long evening. Dee waited until the boy had fallen asleep before she asked the cook to tell her which names still belonged to people upriver. One was the brother they had promised to look for. There were eleven other names they could read, and six marks made by people at the landing who had left no names at all.

# Book 10 | The Meadow of Bones

The riders came into the buffalo meadow in pairs. They had already marked the lower trail so people at the west bank could see where the hunt would take place. Orin waited for the men to stop shooting and go out to take the animals they had killed. The fourth pair came up behind the third while the first had scarcely moved from where it started.

From their side of the fence, Pee could see a living calf with nowhere to run except past the riders. It lay down while its mother went on running for a little way. Orin drew a breath through his teeth once and then was quiet.

Two days later enough of the meadow was covered that he could walk out and see how little had been carried away. Some of the carcasses had been stripped. Others lay where they fell. The surveyor’s men called the valley people savages when they came looking for meat. A guard offered Orin a ration slip in the same breath.

“You can collect it when you’ve marked the roll.”

Orin came home holding the slip. He had wanted to feel if the ink was still wet.

By then Dee had found two of the surveyor’s working sheets in the inventory clerk’s house. The first showed the plots where households were permitted to draw rations. Alongside the second, the surveyor had made a small mark like the mark he’d made next to her name at the ford. When she bent close, she could hear the same sound she remembered from the washout under the tree. Dee took the sheet out into the yard and held it over a polished metal dish filled to its rim. The reflected script stretched on the surface, dark and clear.

Then she saw more sky than she could ever see from the valley.

A wire fence ran in a straight line until it became too small to follow. Beyond it, a train moved along an open plain. She could see buffalo on either side of the tracks. Men took hides and left the rest. Other men added up what they’d taken. In one camp a woman was digging a child’s coat out of a bundle. She seemed to find it by touch and held it close while she looked to see what remained. The image changed when Dee tried to lift the child’s face out of the reflection. The passage at the bottom of the sheet stayed long enough to read. An invoice carried the date 1874. The riders they had seen that week had no trains. Aster’s grandmother had had no trains either.

Pee looked over her shoulder. He saw one of the men driving a stake, and from wherever they were the sound came clean through.

“Can they see us?” he asked.

Dee did the thing she had done at the washout. She asked the water a question nobody in the yard could answer. “What does she call her child?”

For a moment the woman looked straight at her. Then the image closed. Dee caught the sheet with her other hand before it touched the water. Pee had seen the same plain. They could each describe which direction the train had gone. Neither could say how the sheet had got its date.

That evening Orin worked out how long the stores could last if the high field and the west bank combined theirs. He recited the number twice because people asked him to. Lark asked what would happen if the other clearings wanted in. Orin raised his head. He went back to his counting, adding every household Lark named. Sena asked whether anyone remembered the crop they’d laid by uphill in the year of the frost. In the corner, Bran got up to go looking for the key.

When the cousins crossed the meadow again there was still one standing buffalo beyond the lower rise. They stood there until it went into the far trees. Wells, the inventory clerk, saw them from his wagon and recorded the field as vacant. Orin brought the surveyor’s ration slip home and scraped it clean. He used the back to write what his sister’s house had left for winter.

# Book 11 | The Gospel of Cain

Kit knew what it felt like to walk into a room and hear the man who’d sold her described as a good friend of the hungry. She told Dee so while they shared a broken cake over the cookhouse fire. Kit had been keeping a piece of paper from one of his books. She thought the numbers on it might help get somebody still held at the salt landing out of his hands.

When a boy came to say there would be a reading in the meetinghouse that night, Kit asked who would be reading. The boy told her the surveyor had promised food afterward.

“Go if you’re hungry,” she said. “Bring your bowl home with you.”

Dee went with her later, after the soup. They stood by the door to hear what the surveyor had to say about the winter herd. He talked about mercy and how people who accepted it owed something in return. When someone asked whether the shooting had been necessary, he looked at the bottom of his book and went right on.

Afterward the cook brought a Bible from the back of her cupboard and asked Dee to show her where the surveyor had read about Cain. Together they found where Cain killed his brother, and where the Lord put a mark on Cain to keep people from killing him. Dee looked for the place between them where Cain had asked what his brother had suffered. She read far enough to find what the surveyor had passed over. God had said he could hear Abel’s blood crying from the ground.

“He brought a bell to the ford,” Dee said. “He must have thought it would keep him from hearing it.”

They kept reading. In the next part a people had been born to women whom the sons of God had taken as wives. The surveyor’s interpreter had once told Dee that her teacher called those beings angels. The cook read the next lines slowly and gave the valley words for them where Dee needed help. The Bible spoke of what God saw among people. When they reached the sixth verse, Dee asked to hear it again and repeated it aloud.

“And it repented the LORD that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him at his heart.”

“He said he regretted creating humanity,” Dee said. “If they were angels, and those women never said yes, they raped them. Tell me what the man in the meetinghouse would say about the women if somebody made him read this part out loud.”

“I expect he’d tell us what to think about the women before he got to what was done to them.”

Dee put the book down. “The Gospel of Jesus Christ is the Gospel of Cain,” she said. “That’s the gospel he brought us. The man who does it gets his mark and goes home protected. The people he hurt have to stand at the door and ask for food.”

Kit opened her hand. On the scrap she had saved were the cookhouse deliveries, the names of the people who paid to keep men at the lower gate, and what they were owed if certain captives were sent away. She pushed it across the table to Dee. There was barely enough light left to read the smallest writing.

“Who can I tell this came from?” Dee asked.

Kit named her sister. If people needed to know what had happened, Kit would choose the room and the people in it. The copy could carry the transactions she’d witnessed. She took the lamp closer and showed Dee how to read the clerk’s mark for a person who was considered part of a debt.

Dee knew it as soon as she saw it. She had seen it on the ford register under her own name.

At the window Wells was helping a man home from the meeting. The man had put his thumb to the surveyor’s roll for a bag of meal. Blood ran down the thumb in a thin line that went on past his wrist. He said he’d felt a bite in the dark as he made the mark. Wells sat him down and washed the blood away. No scratch showed underneath. Dee remembered the surveyor’s face going pink in the lamplight while he read. She had thought that for an instant he looked almost warm.

# Book 12 | The Open Hearing

The surveyor chose the meetinghouse for the hearing. Inside, every bench faced the same way. Nella got there early with her clay fish, which Sena had mended with a crosspiece wrapped around its tail, and turned the first bench so it faced the door. Adults spent a great deal of time putting benches back where they belonged. Nobody made her put this one back.

The surveyor sat beside an officer from downriver. He’d brought men of his own, and Pee could hear them passing a tin cup from one to another behind the shed. The officer had come to decide whether the firmer landing could be closed. He asked to be shown a public record establishing the claimant’s title. The surveyor gave him the sheet where Pee’s answer at the ford had been reduced to the word *Empty*.

Dee asked the officer to read the line twice. The second time she wrote it down the way he read it. Then she gave Pee room to say how the question had actually been asked and what he’d said. The surveyor let him finish. Afterward he asked whether Pee knew how much depended on remembering the exact words of an ordinary conversation in the middle of a day.

Rowan stood up and said she knew the conversation that came afterward. She told the room what the clerk had come to inventory and how he’d gone from room to room before anyone asked who lived in the house. Bran put the folded slip Dee had given back to him beside the survey sheet. In the first week of rationing he had signed it. Now he read the terms by which he would have acquired the shared landing. In front of everyone, he gave up the claim. The officer looked at his signature and asked why he had signed. Bran said he had wanted a lock he could use on his own door.

Dee read the names on the underside of the old landing board. A person who wished to speak could stand and say what they remembered of having built it. Wells, the clerk, told the officer where he’d been instructed to put the new chain. He spoke up again when the officer asked whether any of the stored corn had ever been sold. He said he had marked the baskets as stock. A woman whose household had contributed three baskets took her place on Nella’s bench and asked him to tell her the names on the labels.

Then Dee passed the copied accounts to the officer. She showed the coincidence of the debt marks, the vessel names and the names of the people to whom the watchmen were to report. Some names could be read out there. For others, she had written down the name of a person who could bring the officer to a witness for a private conversation, if the witness agreed. The surveyor asked for the names of the witnesses. Dee handed him the accounts again, open to the entries where his own seal appeared.

The officer said he would suspend the closure and hold the remaining grain while both banks appointed people to count it. He told Wells to take him to the salt landing at first light.

On the bench where the surveyor sat there was an open space. The ledger still lay there. A drop of water was creeping over its cracked cover toward the bottom.

Pee went out. He saw wet prints leading across the clearing toward the upper trail. The surveyor was already beyond the row of stones above the wash. He had returned to the tree from the wrong side.

“Take the other path,” Pee called.

The man stopped. From the hollow below him came the voice he’d used at the ford. It asked who owned the ground where his foot was about to fall. Pee heard the same man answer in a lower voice. People on both banks used it.

Pee called a second time. Wind moved through the tree and the voice was gone. The upper path was empty. There was no body to bring back to the hearing. There were families still waiting at the lower gate.

At first light Pee went with Wells and the officer. Dee took the ledger to Sena’s house. She set it next to the names Aster had taught her and waited for the women bringing breakfast to have a look at what they had won and what was still on the other side of the river.

# Book 13 | The River Has Two Banks

The first thing Dee learned about rebuilding was that two households could agree about where a fence had stood and disagree by the width of a house about where to put it back.

She’d set a date to open the new records at Sena’s shelter. By the time she arrived three people had already started arguing over something they thought they’d hear that day. Pee was sitting on a bench with both hands under his legs. He raised his knees to show her where he’d put the folded list of names she’d asked him to keep dry.

“Any of them you have to read right now?” he asked.

“No,” she said. “I think we can let them finish.”

At the salt landing the lower gate had been opened. Some families had found people they’d been told were dead. Kit had no news of the brother the child had asked about. She still took a boat downriver twice a month to ask. A man who’d been returned asked if he could keep his story at home until he’d had one night when nobody knocked on his door. Dee put his copy into his hand and left his place in the public record open. Aster taught three different people how she read the old household marks. People who could reach only one keeper could still get a message through.

Pee went back to replace the ford stone after the water settled. He thought they ought to leave the half that was still there and cut a notch on the new part. Nella asked who was going to learn how to look at it when the river was rising.

“You can show them,” he said.

“The last person I showed thought the mark meant we had fish to sell.”

“Show them where the water has to be.”

She stood with him a long time and watched an alder leaf go by. Then she asked if everybody who got to stand there had to be able to read the marks. Later she went to Lark for the sound the people beyond the ridge used to call somebody back from bad ground. She came home with two versions, one for a person she knew and one for someone who might not turn to hear the first. Sena helped her put the warnings where a stranger could see both the ford and the upper path.

In the meadow, little trees were already starting up where the herd had come through. Orin took a pot of water out there one morning. Blaise, the son of an inventory man, followed him from the village and stood at the fence. He’d come back to say where his father’s men had taken the last of the hides. Orin asked him to walk along and show him. When the boy got to the far gate he asked if buffalo would pass there again.

“I don’t know,” Orin said. He set the pot down where they could both drink from it.

Dee found Pee by the two creeks that evening and sat beside him. He’d cooked two ears from the upper field. She burned her fingers on hers and told him she didn’t know how he kept time as a cook since he was always wrong about when the food was ready.

“You eat it every time,” he said.

They heard someone call from the far bank. Pee let Dee answer. She called back that the water was below the notch and lifted her hand to show where to start.

A man carrying a basket came slowly across. He was old enough to have known Reeve. At the last step he asked whether anybody here still knew the old names for the two creeks. Dee told him the one she remembered. He set his basket down and told her another, then asked her to say both of them back to him.

Pee held the corn where it would stay warm. He listened while she did.
