Historical and Textual Notes

The valley before the encounter

The Pee Dee is a real river system. The households, household marks, council decisions, ford stone, and supernatural warnings in this story belong to a fictional community. North Carolina’s Office of State Archaeology documents regional Woodland settlements that cultivated maize, beans and squash while also hunting and gathering. Town Creek Indian Mound provides a regional archaeological point of reference for Indigenous life before colonization. The state historic site’s use of “Pee Dee culture” describes an archaeological classification. The Pee Dee Indian Tribe’s own published history describes a living political community along the river and records a Pee Dee diplomat, Tom West, taking part in negotiations in 1716. The fictional families have no assigned relationship to that archaeological classification or to a present-day tribe.

  • North Carolina Office of State Archaeology · North Carolina’s First Colonists
  • North Carolina Historic Sites · Pee Dee Culture at Town Creek
  • Pee Dee Indian Tribe · Tribal History

Contact and the trade in people

Book 7 depicts the first encounter of two particular cousins, at an unnamed local ford. The series uses a fictional chronology. European contact took different forms at different times in the Southeast. At Joara, in the Catawba watershed of present-day western North Carolina, archaeological and documentary work places Spanish contact in the sixteenth century. John Lawson’s travels began in 1700, and his account was published in 1709. Lawson wrote as an English colonial observer. The College of Charleston’s Lowcountry Digital History Initiative documents the trade in enslaved Indigenous people in colonial Carolina. The Yamassee War began in 1715 amid disputes over trade, debt and abuses. The salt landing, buyers’ records, and hearing in Books 8, 9, 11 and 12 are fictional.

  • Exploring Joara Foundation · Research at the Berry Site
  • Library of Congress · John Lawson’s A New Voyage to Carolina
  • College of Charleston · American Indian Slavery in Carolina
  • South Carolina Encyclopedia · Yamassee War

The buffalo in two places

North Carolina Parks documents historical bison in parts of the state, extending at least to the central Piedmont. The particular herd and the deliberate slaughter in Pee and Dee’s meadow are inventions. The dated Plains glimpse in Book 10 is a fictional scene set in 1874, during the documented nineteenth-century mass slaughter. Commercial hide hunting and expanding rail access helped drive the slaughter. Federal and military officials also treated the destruction of bison as a way to deprive Plains nations of a major food source. The reflection lets the cousins witness a different region and era during their own lifetimes. The people glimpsed in it belong to that scene and have their own lives beyond the cousins’ view.

  • North Carolina Parks · Historical Distribution of American Bison
  • National Park Service · What Happened to the Bison
  • Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian · Before the 1833 Treaty

Cain and the Watchers

Genesis 4:8–15 in the King James Version recounts Abel’s killing, God hearing Abel’s blood cry from the ground, Cain’s punishment, and the mark protecting Cain from being killed. Genesis 6:1–7 calls the figures “sons of God,” says they took wives, and records God’s grief at having made humanity. Dee reads the taking of the women as rape and the sons of God as angels. The ancient Book of the Watchers in 1 Enoch expands the angelic reading and recounts judgment on the Watchers. Dee’s accusation in Book 11 is directed at the surveyor and the gospel he uses to justify his protection and his conduct.

  • King James Version · Genesis 4–8
  • Religious Studies Center · Enoch in the Old Testament and Beyond

The warning at the tree

The tree, the stone, the echo and the rules for containing it are fictional. The hazard and the warning are different things in Book 3. A familiar sign fails a visitor, so the people who know the danger make the safer route visible and explain it in person. The comparison with traveling workers’ warning signs inspires that design question. Evidence for a single standardized historical hobo sign system is disputed.

  • National Cryptologic Museum · Hobo Communications
  • Historic Graffiti Society · Hobo Signs
