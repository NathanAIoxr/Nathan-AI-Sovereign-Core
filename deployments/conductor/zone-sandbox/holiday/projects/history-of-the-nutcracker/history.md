# The History of the Nutcracker

The nutcracker began with a practical problem. How do you break a hard shell without crushing the food inside? A hammerstone delivered a blow. A hinged tool let the hand squeeze the shell. A screw press let the user tighten it gradually. The German wooden soldier took the lever and made it part of a character. The handle became its back, and the jaws became its mouth. ## About 780,000 years ago — Hammerstone and anvil

At Gesher Benot Ya’aqov, near the Jordan River in what is now Israel, archaeologists found remains of seven kinds of edible nuts alongside an assemblage of 54 pitted stones. The finds included wild almonds, pistachios, and acorns. The researchers compared the wear on the stones with marks left by nut cracking. The evidence points to early hominins using a hammerstone to strike nuts against a stone anvil. The shape of a pit alone leaves room for other uses, including pounding other foods. [Nut cracking study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC122386/) · [Dating and plant remains](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5187744/) · [Follow-up anvil study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26483531/)

## Around 300 B.C. — The bronze hands of Taranto

A tomb at Contrada Rondinella in Taranto, a Greek city in what is now southern Italy, held one of the earliest surviving metal nutcrackers. The piece depicts two bronze forearms and clasped hands. Gold-foil bracelets coil around the wrists and end in snake heads. A hinge between the palms lets the hands move against each other, and pins once held a handle at the base of the forearms. The National Archaeological Museum of Taranto identifies it as a table utensil. Its burial may also have carried a ritual meaning, which the museum leaves open. Claims that the hands served as a torture instrument or bedroom device remain speculative. [Taranto museum](https://museotaranto.cultura.gov.it/en/objects-on-display/nutcracker-in-bronze-and-gold-foil/)

The Leavenworth Nutcracker Museum also holds a bronze implement dated broadly to **200 B.C.–A.D. 200**, reportedly found at a Roman villa between Rome and Naples. The museum identifies cracking hard shells as one possible use. [Leavenworth museum](https://www.nutcrackermuseum.com/Newsletter58.html)

## The 1400s–1700s — Metal levers, carved wood, and screws

The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds an early 15th-century copper-alloy nutcracker made in German or South Netherlandish lands. Leavenworth holds a French boxwood example from around 1500, carved with angels, a bird, and a gargoyle. The London Museum holds a turned wooden screw nutcracker dated to the 17th or 18th century. By then, the kitchen tool had two established mechanical forms. The lever closed jaws around the shell, and the screw increased pressure as it turned. Surviving thumbscrews document the use of screw pressure in torture. The Taranto museum's record identifies a hinged table tool and leaves the meaning of its burial open. ## The 1800s — The wooden king takes shape

Carved nutcracker figures circulated in German-speaking lands before the familiar Ore Mountains design took shape. In **1816**, E. T. A. Hoffmann made a Christmas nutcracker the central figure in *The Nutcracker and the Mouse King*. In **1851**, a different writer, Heinrich Hoffmann, published a picture book about a nutcracker king. Around **1870**, Wilhelm Friedrich Füchtner began turning wooden nutcrackers on a lathe in Seiffen. The lathe let his workshop repeat the same shape, and his painted king in uniform became a model for the regional craft. Makers also portrayed soldiers, miners, and policemen. Some figures were outright caricatures, including a Thuringian Napoleon from around 1820. ## 1892 onward — The ballet makes it a Christmas tradition

Alexandre Dumas adapted E. T. A. Hoffmann’s story into French in **1845**. That version became the basis for Tchaikovsky’s ballet, which premiered at the Mariinsky Theatre in St. Petersburg on **December 18, 1892**. San Francisco Ballet staged the first complete U.S. production on **Christmas Eve 1944**, and New York City Ballet premiered George Balanchine’s version in **1954**. The German workshops gave the wooden figure its familiar shape. The story put it under the Christmas tree, and recurring holiday performances made that scene a tradition.
